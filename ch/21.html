Part 6 . Diagnosis and reference

21Cheat sheet, measure library and review checklist

Everything the previous twenty chapters proved, compressed into tables you can read at speed while a report is broken and someone is waiting. Symptom to cause to fix, the complete Meridian measure library, the filter modifiers, and a fifteen point review you run before you publish.

It is twenty past four. The finance manager needs the month's labour cost for a call at five. The production controller wants to know why the closed work order count moved since Friday. And the report you published on Monday has a blank row in it that nobody can explain. You have forty minutes and no time to reread three chapters.

That is what this chapter is for. It teaches nothing new. It exists so that you do not have to reread a chapter to remember which modifier keeps a filter and which one throws it away. Read the first table when something is wrong. Read the measure library when you are writing DAX, the formula language Power BI uses for measures, and you want a form already known to return the right number against the Meridian rows. Everything here matches the answer key exactly. If you build the model and a measure returns something different, look at the model first. These definitions are the tested ones.

The symptom table

Take the blank row first, because that is the one on your screen. Somebody in the meeting pointed at it, asked what it was, and you had nothing to say. Twelve hours of work on N338WA are sitting inside it. Here is how you find that out in under a minute.

Filter the visual down to the blank member and look at the raw keys behind it. Within seconds you will know which of four things you have: whitespace on one side of a key, a key with no dimension member, a date outside the calendar, or a source column that arrived genuinely empty.

Order matters in the third column. The first thing to check is the one that splits the possible causes roughly in half, chosen for how fast it rules things out. Work down the column in order.

Two words in the table are worth pinning down first. Grain is what one row of a table stands for, written as a sentence: one row of FactLabor is one technician on one work order on one day. Semi additive describes a number you may add across some things and never across others. A forecast revision adds across work orders. It must never be added across the revisions of one work order, because each revision replaces the one before it.

Common mistake

Reaching for the fix column before the check column. Half the entries in this table share a symptom with another entry, and the wrong fix applied confidently is worse than no fix, because it removes the evidence. If a blank row disappears after you switch a relationship to bidirectional, you have not solved anything. You have hidden 12.0 hours of work on N338WA behind a setting nobody will look at again.

The Meridian measure library

The finance manager wants one number for the month and does not care how you get it. He asks for the cost of the work. You open the file and find three measures with cost in the name, two of them written by somebody who has left, and the three disagree by thousands. He is waiting.

That is the situation this library removes. Every measure below is written against the finished model built in Chapter 3 and returns the figure stated underneath it. Names are stable across the whole document. Where a measure has a variant that is deliberately wrong, the wrong version stays in the chapter that discusses it. This library carries only the forms that return the right number. Store all of them in a dedicated measures table with no columns, so the field list groups them away from the data.

This is the model diagram from Chapter 3, repeated here so that you do not have to page back for it. Keep it next to you while you read the library. Every measure below assumes these twelve tables and these joins, with two exceptions. The six heavy maintenance measures reach into three extension tables the figure does not draw, and the eight manpower planning measures after them reach into four more. When a number fails to reproduce, the first question is whether your model has the same shape. Two details in the figure explain most of the awkward measures that follow. The first is the dashed curve running from DimDate across to FactCapacity, labelled month grain only, see chapter 16. The second is the pair of lines that meet at BridgeTechCert along the bottom. One drops into it from DimTechnician above, one arrives from DimCertification to its right, and that meeting point is where a technician's hours get counted once per certification held. The single straight line from DimDate down into FactWorkOrder is the OpenedDate role. The two inactive roles are not drawn at all, because an inactive relationship moves no filter until a measure asks for it.

Base measures

Seven measures cover the additive quantities in the model. Every other measure in this library is built from these or from the same pattern. If one of these returns the wrong number, stop and fix the model before writing anything else.

Total hours booked to labour lines.

DAXLabor Hours
Labor Hours =
    SUM ( FactLabor[Hours] )
Returns
62.0 with no filter. MSP 34.5, TUS 22.5, LAS 5.0. It is additive on every dimension in the model, which is what makes it safe as the base of everything else.

Count of timesheet lines, useful as a denominator and as a sanity check.

DAXLabor Entries
Labor Entries =
    COUNTROWS ( FactLabor )
Returns
8. Chosen over COUNT(FactLabor[LaborID]) because COUNTROWS counts rows including any with a blank in the column you happened to name, and it does not need a column at all.

Labour cost computed at line grain, then summed.

DAXLabor Cost
Labor Cost =
    SUMX (
        FactLabor,
        FactLabor[Hours] * FactLabor[HourlyRate]
    )
Returns
6,326.50. Line by line: 712.50, 660.00, 855.00, 704.00, 495.00, 1,020.00, 1,440.00, 440.00.
Choice
SUMX here. SUM([Hours]) * SUM([HourlyRate]) would give 62.0 times 808, which is meaningless. The multiplication has to happen inside the row.

Parts cost including the orphan issue line, which is the honest stores total.

DAXParts Cost
Parts Cost =
    SUMX (
        FactParts,
        FactParts[Qty] * FactParts[UnitCost]
    )
Returns
28,245 across seven issue lines. By category: Brakes 3,720, Hydraulics 7,350, Engine 15,300, Avionics 1,875.

Quoted value of the work, from the work order header.

DAXQuoted Amount
Quoted Amount =
    SUM ( FactWorkOrder[QuotedAmount] )
Returns
38,100 across eight work orders. Header grain, and as a measure it stays correct in any visual, including one that lists labour lines underneath. What it will not do there is look correct. WO-1001 shows 3,000 against both of its labour rows, so a reader who adds the visible column by hand reaches 46,600 while the total row still reads 38,100.

Work order count at header grain.

DAXWork Order Count
Work Order Count =
    COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder )
Returns
8. By opened month: Jan 3, Feb 3, Mar 2. This counts headers, so it never fans out even when the visual also carries a technician.

Planned capacity, summed from the unpivoted capacity fact.

DAXPlanned Hours
Planned Hours =
    SUM ( FactCapacity[PlannedHours] )
Returns
176 over 28 rows. By station: MSP 90, TUS 50, LAS 20, PHX 16. By trade: Airframe 80, Avionics 44, Engine 39, NDT 13.

Cost measures

Cost is where the finance manager's three disagreeing numbers come from, and where the orphan in PartsIssued starts to matter. P-9007 charges 4,900 against WO-1009, and WO-1009 does not exist. Two different totals are both defensible. Carry both in the model, under names that say which is which, so that nobody has to guess what a visual is showing. What he gets is Total Cost Attributable at 29,671.50, with Orphan Parts Cost at 4,900 on the page beside it.

Parts cost restricted to lines that resolve to a real work order.

DAXParts Cost Attributable
Parts Cost Attributable =
    SUMX (
        FILTER (
            FactParts,
            NOT ISBLANK (
                RELATED ( FactWorkOrder[WorkOrderID] )
            )
        ),
        FactParts[Qty] * FactParts[UnitCost]
    )
Returns
23,345, which is 28,245 less the 4,900 charged to WO-1009.
Choice
RELATED here. A relationship already exists from FactWorkOrder to FactParts, and RELATED follows it directly. LOOKUPVALUE would rescan the header table for every one of the seven lines and would still be wrong the day a work order number repeats.

The cost that fell out of the report, published as a number of its own.

DAXOrphan Parts Cost
Orphan Parts Cost =
    [Parts Cost] - [Parts Cost Attributable]
Returns
4,900. Put this on the data quality page. A number that is visible gets fixed at source. A number hidden inside a difference does not.

Total cost that can be charged against work actually on the books.

DAXActual Cost
Actual Cost =
    [Labor Cost] + [Parts Cost Attributable]
Returns
29,671.50, which is 6,326.50 plus 23,345. WO-1003 alone accounts for 13,879.00 of it.
Earlier form
Chapter 10 and Chapter 16 wrote this as [Labor Cost] + [Parts Cost], because neither chapter had built Parts Cost Attributable yet. The two agree on every work order row, since the orphan line belongs to no work order. They differ by 4,900 on the grand total, where the earlier form reads 34,571.50 and this one reads 29,671.50. The library keeps the attributable version so that the total reconciles against the rows above it, and keeps Orphan Parts Cost beside it so that the 4,900 is still reported somewhere.

Quote against actual, positive when the shop kept money.

DAXQuote Variance
Quote Variance =
    [Quoted Amount] - [Actual Cost]
Returns
38,100 less 29,671.50 gives 8,428.50 across the quarter. Two work orders are negative: WO-1002 by 105.00 and WO-1003 by 1,379.00.
Caution
WO-1004 and WO-1008 are still open, so their variance of 2,980.00 and 5,000.00 is not profit. Filter to closed work orders before anyone reads this as margin.

The same variance as a proportion of what was quoted.

DAXQuote Variance Pct
Quote Variance Pct =
    DIVIDE ( [Quote Variance], [Quoted Amount] )
Returns
8,428.50 divided by 38,100 is 0.2212, so 22.1 percent at the all up level.
Choice
DIVIDE here. A work order with a zero quote makes the slash operator raise an error, and that error propagates through every subtotal above it. DIVIDE returns blank and the visual keeps working.

Work order measures with role playing dates

The production controller has two pages open, built by two different people. One says February had 3 work orders. The other says February had 2. He wants to know which of his colleagues got it wrong. The answer is neither of them.

DimDate joins FactWorkOrder three times. That arrangement is called a role playing date: one calendar table, serving more than one date column on the same fact. OpenedDate is the active role. PromisedDate and ClosedDate are inactive, meaning they carry no filter until a measure asks for them by name. Each of the three measures below is the same count with a different relationship switched on, which is why they must never be given generic names. February has 3 opened, 2 closed and 3 promised, and the only work order that appears in all three sets is WO-1005. Both of the controller's pages are right. Both are missing one word in the title.

Work orders opened in the period, using the active relationship.

DAXWork Orders Opened
// The active DimDate[Date] to OpenedDate relationship applies
Work Orders Opened =
    COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder )
Returns
Jan 3, Feb 3, Mar 2, total 8. No modifier needed, which is exactly why the default role has to be the one the business asks about most often.

Work orders closed in the period, switching to the inactive ClosedDate join.

DAXWork Orders Closed
Work Orders Closed =
    CALCULATE (
        COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder ),
        USERELATIONSHIP (
            DimDate[Date],
            FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate]
        ),
        NOT ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
    )
Returns
Jan 2, Feb 2, Mar 2, total 6.
Why the predicate
Drop the NOT ISBLANK line and the monthly figures stay right while the total becomes 8. USERELATIONSHIP only changes which relationship a date filter travels down. On a grand total row there is no date filter to travel, so nothing excludes WO-1004 and WO-1008, and the measure quietly counts two work orders that have never closed.
Choice
USERELATIONSHIP here. A second copy of DimDate would also work. Every date slicer, every time intelligence measure and every drill path would then have to be built twice, and users would face two fields called Month.

Work orders promised in the period, which is the commitment view of the same eight work orders.

DAXWork Orders Promised
Work Orders Promised =
    CALCULATE (
        COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder ),
        USERELATIONSHIP (
            DimDate[Date],
            FactWorkOrder[PromisedDate]
        )
    )
Returns
Jan 2, Feb 3, Mar 3, total 8. Every work order has a promise, including the two that are still open, so this total matches the opened total while the monthly split does not.

Work orders with no close date, at whatever date the report is filtered to.

DAXOpen Work Orders
Open Work Orders =
    CALCULATE (
        COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder ),
        ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
    )
Returns
2, being WO-1004 and WO-1008.
Choice
A boolean predicate inside CALCULATE here. The predicate names one column, so the engine can turn it into a column filter and never materialise the table. FILTER over the whole fact would work on eight rows and would not work on eight million.

The complement, kept as its own measure so that ratios have a stable denominator.

DAXClosed Work Orders
Closed Work Orders =
    CALCULATE (
        COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder ),
        NOT ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
    )
Returns
6. Every on time and turn time measure below divides by this, never by Work Order Count, because an open work order has no turn time to average.
Not the same as
Work Orders Closed, above. That one counts work orders whose close date falls inside the selected period. This one counts work orders that have a close date at all, inside whatever the active OpenedDate relationship has already selected. Both read 6 on an unfiltered page. Slice to January and Work Orders Closed returns 2, being WO-1001 and WO-1002, while Closed Work Orders returns 3, being the three January openings that have since closed.

Average days from open to close, closed work orders only.

DAXAvg Turn Time Days
Avg Turn Time Days =
    AVERAGEX (
        FILTER (
            FactWorkOrder,
            NOT ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
        ),
        DATEDIFF (
            FactWorkOrder[OpenedDate],
            FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate],
            DAY
        )
    )
Returns
12.0 days. The six values are 10, 5, 22, 2, 30 and 3, summing to 72 over six work orders.
Choice
DATEDIFF here. Subtracting one date from the other returns a decimal number that carries any time component along with it, and it gives you no place to state the unit. DATEDIFF forces you to write DAY, which is the thing a reviewer needs to see.
Choice
AVERAGEX here. AVERAGE over a stored turn days column gives the same 12.0. The iterator version keeps the open work orders out of the denominator without needing a second column to hold the exclusion.

Work orders closed on or before the promised date.

DAXOn Time Work Orders
On Time Work Orders =
    COUNTROWS (
        FILTER (
            FactWorkOrder,
            NOT ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
                && FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate]
                    <= FactWorkOrder[PromisedDate]
        )
    )
Returns
3, being WO-1001, WO-1003 and WO-1005.
Choice
FILTER here. The condition compares two columns of the same table, and a boolean predicate inside CALCULATE may reference only one column. This is one of the few places where the iterator is not optional.

On time delivery as a share of work actually completed.

DAXOn Time Pct
On Time Pct =
    DIVIDE ( [On Time Work Orders], [Closed Work Orders] )
Returns
3 divided by 6, so 50.0 percent. Against all eight work orders it would read 37.5 percent, which would be a different and less useful claim.

Total days late across the quarter, counting only overruns.

DAXDays Late
Days Late =
    SUMX (
        FILTER (
            FactWorkOrder,
            NOT ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] )
        ),
        MAX (
            DATEDIFF (
                FactWorkOrder[PromisedDate],
                FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate],
                DAY
            ),
            0
        )
    )
Returns
9 days: WO-1002 by 3, WO-1006 by 5, WO-1007 by 1, and zero for the three that were on time.
Choice
The two argument form of MAX here. It compares two scalars and reads as a floor, so early deliveries contribute nothing instead of subtracting from the lateness of others. An IF says the same thing in more words.

Closures accumulated from the start of the year, or year to date, which is the one time intelligence measure this model needs. The name carries the abbreviation because that is what a reader scanning a field list expects to see.

DAXWork Orders Closed YTD
Work Orders Closed YTD =
    CALCULATE (
        [Work Orders Closed],
        DATESYTD ( DimDate[Date] )
    )
Returns
Jan 2, Feb 4, Mar 6. The quarter is the whole year in this dataset, so March and the grand total agree at 6.
Choice
DATESYTD rather than TOTALYTD. Both give the same six numbers. DATESYTD returns a table of dates that you hand to CALCULATE yourself. The role swap then sits where you can see it, inside the measure being shifted. TOTALYTD buries it in a wrapper function's second argument.
Order
Call the measure, do not inline its definition into a variable here. A variable holding the closed count is resolved before CALCULATE shifts the dates, so the shift would apply to a number that has already been computed and every month would read 2, 2, 2 again.
Cost
This returns blank on every row if DimDate is not marked as a date table, or if the calendar has a gap. It gives no error and no warning, which is why the marked date table check sits in the review list below.
Performance note

Three of the measures above wrap FILTER(FactWorkOrder, ...). On eight rows that is free. On a fact with tens of millions of rows, FILTER over the table forces a row by row scan with the full row context available. A single column predicate inside CALCULATE can often be pushed down into the storage engine instead. When you migrate these patterns to a real shop floor extract, rewrite anything that filters on one column, and leave the two column comparisons alone because they have no alternative.

Utilisation

The planner clicks 15 January on the date slicer and the utilisation card goes blank. She clicks 1 January and it comes back. She has decided the report only works on the first of the month, and that is closer to the truth than she realises.

Utilisation is the hardest thing in this model, and the DAX is the easy part of it. Labour is recorded per technician per day. Capacity is planned per trade per station per month. The relationship on MonthStart works when the report is filtered to a whole month or coarser. On any single day that is not the first of a month, it returns blank. The measure below papers over that, and you need to know exactly what it is doing before you put it in front of that planner.

Capacity for whatever months the current date filter touches.

DAXPlanned Hours Month Safe
Planned Hours Month Safe =
    CALCULATE (
        SUM ( FactCapacity[PlannedHours] ),
        REMOVEFILTERS ( DimDate ),
        TREATAS (
            VALUES ( DimDate[MonthStart] ),
            FactCapacity[MonthStart]
        )
    )
Line 4
REMOVEFILTERS(DimDate) drops the day level filter that the physical relationship cannot use.
Line 5
TREATAS puts the filter back at month grain. Filter arguments are evaluated in the context outside CALCULATE, so VALUES(DimDate[MonthStart]) still sees the original selection and returns 2025-01-01 for any day in January.
Returns
176 unfiltered. MSP January 30, TUS January 25, LAS March 20, PHX March 16.
Choice
TREATAS here. It applies an existing single column table as a filter without scanning the fact, and it does not require the relationship to be usable at day grain. FILTER(ALL(FactCapacity), ...) scans the whole fact to do the same job.
Cost
Filter to 2025-01-15 alone and this returns a full month of capacity against one day of labour. There is no honest daily number, because the source has none. Put "capacity is monthly" in the visual title and mean it. The planner's 15 January click now returns a figure, and the title tells her it is a month of capacity standing behind one day of work.

Hours booked against hours planned.

DAXUtilisation Pct
Utilisation Pct =
    DIVIDE ( [Labor Hours], [Planned Hours Month Safe] )
Returns
62.0 over 176, so 35.2 percent overall. MSP January 22.5 over 30 is 75.0 percent, TUS January 50.0 percent, MSP February 40.0 percent, TUS February 40.0 percent, LAS March 25.0 percent, MSP March and PHX March both zero.
Earlier name
Chapter 2 and Chapter 7 call this Utilisation and divide by the plain Planned Hours. Every figure above is identical under either denominator, because both read the same 176 planned hours at month grain or coarser. They part company only below month grain, where the plain Planned Hours returns blank for any day that is not the first of a month and Planned Hours Month Safe returns the whole month. Chapter 16 sets out four ways to handle that, and this measure is the second of them. That chapter folds the same TREATAS step into Planned Hours itself, keyed on MonthKey instead of MonthStart, and then keeps a guarded Utilisation that refuses to answer below month grain.

Planned hours at station and month combinations where nobody booked any time.

DAXIdle Planned Hours
Idle Planned Hours =
    SUMX (
        SUMMARIZE (
            FactCapacity,
            DimStation[StationCode],
            DimDate[MonthStart]
        ),
        VAR Planned = [Planned Hours]
        VAR Booked = [Labor Hours]
        RETURN
            IF ( Booked = 0, Planned, 0 )
    )
Returns
46 hours: MSP March 30 plus PHX March 16. Both are planned capacity that produced no recorded work.
Choice
SUMMARIZE over columns of DimStation and DimDate here. Grouping on the fact's own columns would give the right list of pairs. The engine then turns the row the iterator is standing on into a filter, a move called context transition, and that filter would reach FactCapacity only. FactLabor would stay unfiltered, because filters do not travel from the many side to the one side.

Heavy maintenance planning

The planner froze a baseline for WO-1003 weeks before the aircraft arrived: 13.0 hours of work. The check was released on 2025-02-05 having taken 12.5. Somewhere in between, production control revised the forecast to 16.5. Three numbers, one work order, and a meeting in which all three people believe theirs is the manhour figure.

Six measures settle that meeting. Nothing else in the library depends on them, and they depend on three tables the diagram above does not draw, because Meridian sends nine extracts and the diagram covers six. HMCCSchedule is the control board kept by the Heavy Maintenance Control Centre, one row per work order, and it is the only place ServiceLine and CheckType exist at all. Its four service lines are Heavy, Light, Line, and Cabin and IFE, where IFE stands for in-flight entertainment. It is merged into FactWorkOrder in Power Query. One row on each side makes it a one to one, and a one to one is a merge waiting to happen. HeavyPreInput is the pre-input pack, the tab a planner fills in weeks before the aircraft arrives, at one row per work order per trade, loaded as FactPreInput with 8 rows. ManhourForecast is production control's re-forecast, one row per work order per revision, loaded as FactForecast with 8 rows. Chapter B builds all six of these from the source rows and argues for each choice. What follows is the reference copy.

The three numbers underneath them are available, projected and achieved. Learn those three words, because this section uses them and nothing else. Available is what the pre-input pack said the work should take, frozen at induction so that performance has something stable to be measured against. That is the planner's 13.0. Projected is the current estimate of the final total, revised as findings land. A technician opens a panel, finds corrosion that no routine task card covers, raises a non-routine card, and the number moves. That is production control's 16.5. Each revision supersedes the one before it, so revisions are never added together. Achieved is booked time from the timesheet system, and of the three it is the one that records something that actually happened. That is the shop floor's 12.5. The function that owns the second number is that control centre, HMCC for short. Treat those four letters with more suspicion than you treat AOG, which means the same thing in every shop. Other shops call the identical function production control or the base maintenance control centre, and the line maintenance equivalent is usually MOC, Maintenance Operations Control.

The planner's frozen baseline, summed from the pre-input pack.

DAXAvailable Hours
Available Hours =
    SUM ( FactPreInput[PlannedHours] )
Returns
57.0 over eight rows. By work order, WO-1001 13.0, WO-1003 13.0, WO-1004 12.0, WO-1006 10.0 and WO-1008 9.0. By trade, Airframe 34.0, Avionics 4.0, Engine 5.0, Cabin 12.0 and NDT 2.0.
Choice
SUM here, for the same reason Planned Hours uses it. Each row is one trade on one work order, so no row overlaps another and available is fully additive across every dimension in the model. SUMX would add a scan and change nothing.
Watch the name
FactPreInput[PlannedHours] and FactCapacity[PlannedHours] are two different columns with one name. The first is demand, what the work needs. The second is supply, what the shop can staff. Utilisation compares them, so rename one of the two in Power Query before somebody drags the wrong field onto a chart and gets a number that looks reasonable.

Booked time, under the name production control reads it in.

DAXAchieved Hours
Achieved Hours =
    [Labor Hours]
Returns
62.0 unfiltered. By service line, Heavy 38.0, Cabin and IFE 10.0, Light 9.0 plus Line 5.0. Restricted to the five work orders that have a pre-input pack it is 48.0, and 48.0 plus 14.0 ties back to the 62.0 that every other measure in this library reports.
Same measure
Chapter B writes this out as SUM(FactLabor[Hours]), which is the definition of Labor Hours at the top of this library. Two definitions of one expression is a defect. One definition carrying a second name is fine, and the second name earns its place here. Put Labor Hours in a matrix beside Available Hours and Projected Hours, and the reader takes it for a different kind of number.

The current estimate of the final total, taking the newest revision of each work order and discarding the rest.

DAXProjected Hours
Projected Hours =
    SUMX (
        VALUES ( FactForecast[WorkOrderID] ),
        VAR LatestRevision =
            CALCULATE ( MAX ( FactForecast[RevisionDate] ) )
        RETURN
            CALCULATE (
                SUM ( FactForecast[ProjectedHours] ),
                FactForecast[RevisionDate] = LatestRevision
            )
    )
Line 3
VALUES returns the work orders visible in the current filter context. Five of them here, because only five work orders were ever forecast.
Lines 4 and 5
The bare CALCULATE performs a context transition. It turns the work order the iterator is standing on into a filter, so MAX returns the latest revision date for that one work order. An unfiltered MAX would return the latest date in the whole table.
Lines 7 to 10
The inner CALCULATE keeps the work order and replaces the date filter with the single date found above. One row survives per work order, and SUMX adds the five.
Returns
63.5, being WO-1001 14.0, WO-1003 16.5, WO-1004 12.0, WO-1006 13.5 and WO-1008 7.5. A plain SUM of the same table returns 99.5, which adds superseded revisions to live ones and describes nothing that ever happened.
Choice
MAX of the revision date here. MAX of the projected hours also returns 63.5, because every Meridian revision happens to move upwards, and it fails the first day a controller cuts a forecast after a finding turns out to be cosmetic. A measure that is right for the wrong reason cannot be tested, because the test passes. Filtering on the text Rev 1 is worse again: it loses the three work orders that were never revised, and it sorts Rev 10 ahead of Rev 2 the moment a check runs long.
Cost
This is semi additive, so the date selection decides which revision is the latest one, assuming you joined RevisionDate to the calendar. Slice to March and only the two work orders revised in March survive, 13.5 plus 7.5, so the card reads 21.0 and looks like the workload collapsed. That is arguably right, in that it answers what production control thought during March, and arguably wrong on a card labelled Projected Hours. Decide which, then say so on the visual. Chapter 16 covers the family.

The planner graded against the shop floor: what the plan said, over what the work took.

DAXPerformance Factor
// above 100 percent means the work came in under the
// frozen plan. Confirm the direction locally.
Performance Factor =
    DIVIDE ( [Available Hours], [Achieved Hours] )
Returns
94.7 percent across the three closed heavy checks, being 36.0 available over 38.0 achieved. WO-1001 reads 96.3 percent, WO-1003 104.0 percent and WO-1006 83.3 percent.
Direction
Plenty of shops invert this and divide achieved by available, so that a figure above 100 percent is bad news rather than good. Neither convention is more correct and both are in use. Ask production control which way they read it, write the answer into the measure description, and never assume the report you inherited used your convention.
Scope
Filter to closed heavy checks before anyone sees it. On an unfiltered page it divides 57.0 by 62.0 and reads 91.9 percent, which sets a plan that excludes light maintenance against hours that include it. The number is arithmetically fine and answers no question.
Choice
DIVIDE with no third argument, so a work order with no pre-input pack comes back blank instead of zero. Supply a zero and the three light work orders report a performance factor of zero percent, which reads as a team that achieved nothing against its plan. They had no plan. Those are different statements and only one of them starts an argument you cannot win.

Production control graded instead: what the work took, over what the last forecast said it would take.

DAXForecast Accuracy
// measured at close. Below 100 percent means the last
// forecast was too high.
Forecast Accuracy =
    DIVIDE ( [Achieved Hours], [Projected Hours] )
Returns
86.4 percent across the same three closed checks, being 38.0 achieved over 44.0 projected. WO-1001 reads 96.4 percent, WO-1003 75.8 percent and WO-1006 88.9 percent.
Read it with the one above
WO-1003 scores 104.0 percent on performance and 75.8 percent on forecast accuracy. The plan was sound, the check beat it, and the forecast written in between said 16.5 against an eventual 12.5, so it was 4.0 hours too high. That is a forecasting problem, and the pair of measures is what separates it from an execution problem. Either one on its own would have blamed the wrong department. That is the WO-1003 argument settled. The planner's 13.0 was sound, the shop beat it at 12.5, and the 16.5 written in between is the number that needs explaining.
Scope
Closed work only. On an open check the numerator is still growing, so the ratio starts near zero and climbs all week, and anybody reading it before the aircraft leaves is reading progress as accuracy.

What the current forecast still expects to be booked on work that has not finished.

DAXRemaining To Go
Remaining To Go =
    VAR Forecast = [Projected Hours]
    VAR Booked = [Achieved Hours]
    VAR IsOpen =
        ISBLANK (
            CALCULATE ( MAX ( FactWorkOrder[ClosedDate] ) )
        )
    RETURN
        IF (
            IsOpen && NOT ISBLANK ( Forecast ),
            Forecast - Booked
        )
Lines 2 and 3
Evaluate each measure once and hold it. Both are referenced below, and the forecast selection is the expensive one to repeat.
Lines 4 to 7
A work order with no close date is still in the hangar. ISBLANK of the maximum close date in the current context is true for WO-1004 and WO-1008 and false for the other six.
Lines 9 to 12
Two guards, and only one of them earns its keep against these eight rows. Drop the open test and closed checks report work still to come: WO-1003 would show 16.5 less 12.5, which is 4.0 hours outstanding on an aircraft released on 2025-02-05. The blank test changes nothing here, because every light work order is already closed and the open test catches it first. Keep it anyway. It is there for the first quarter in which a light job is still in the hangar at the cutoff. A blank forecast less 9.0 booked hours evaluates to minus 9.0, and the card then reports negative work outstanding.
Returns
9.5 hours, being 2.0 on WO-1004 (12.0 less 10.0) and 7.5 on WO-1008 (7.5 less 0.0). Without the guards the same subtraction totals 15.5 across the five forecast work orders, and 6.0 of that belongs to checks that were released weeks ago.
Choice
An IF with the else branch left out, so the result comes back blank. Zero would put a closed check on the same footing as an open check that is running exactly to forecast, and a production controller reads those two cells very differently at eight in the morning.
Rule of thumb

None of these words is standardised. Available is planned in one shop, budgeted in the next and sold in a third, and sold is the figure the customer was quoted, which is allowed to differ from the internal estimate on purpose. Projected is also called forecast, or estimate at completion. Achieved is actual, booked, expended or clocked. The same word can mean the frozen baseline in one report and the live forecast in the next, inside the same building. Before you write any of these six measures against a real extract, write down the local definition of each term. Get somebody in production control to agree it in writing. Paste it into the measure description, so that the definition travels with the model instead of living in your head.

Manpower planning

The cabin line missed February by one hour. The production controller wants to know why. The shift supervisor says his crew worked every hour it was given, and the planner who set the roster back in January has already moved on to March. All three of them are right, and the eight measures below are how you show that in a meeting instead of arguing about it.

These measures come from the resource planning side of the shop, which sends its own set of extracts again. The heavy maintenance section above took Meridian from six extracts to nine. Manpower planning sends six more, and five of them stay as tables of their own. RPODemand is what the central resource planning function asked the shop to deliver, at one row per service line per month, six rows and 75.0 hours. CrewRoster is attended hours, meaning hours people are rostered to be present and paid. It sits at the same service line and month grain, six rows and 109.0 hours. ProductivityFactor holds one row per service line, carrying the fraction of attended time that reaches a job card. DeptSubmission is the official month end figure each department reports upward, six rows and 63.0 hours. CrewCodeMap is the crew code to service line mapping you maintain by hand. The sixth file, CrewAssignment, says which crew each technician belongs to, and it merges onto DimTechnician because one row per technician is a one to one. Chapter C builds the planning chain from these rows and Chapter D builds the crew route. What follows is the reference copy.

The factor is the one number in this chain that people argue about, so read it slowly. Meridian books 0.88 for heavy and 0.68 for line, for light, and for cabin and IFE. Those two values are illustrative and they belong to Meridian. Every shop sets its own and reviews them, and heavy normally runs higher because a hangar crew works a continuous shift on one aircraft while a line crew waits for arrivals. The factor absorbs briefings, tool collection, travel to stand, waiting for access and paperwork. All of that is paid time. None of it lands on a job card.

What the resource planning function asked the shop to deliver.

DAXDemand Hours
// what the business asked for, before any roster exists
Demand Hours =
    SUM ( RPODemand[DemandHours] )
Returns
75.0 over six rows. Heavy 48.0, being 30.0 in January, 14.0 in February and 4.0 in March. Light 10.0, cabin and IFE 11.0, line 6.0.
Why SUM
One row per service line per month, and nothing on the row needs multiplying before it is added. SUMX would add a scan and return the same 75.0.
Cost
Demand is published for six service line and month combinations only. Filter to light in February and the measure comes back blank, because the planning function asked for no light work that month. Blank there means not asked for. A visual that prints zero instead is making a claim the source never made.

Hours the roster says people will be present and paid for.

DAXAttended Hours
// hours people are rostered to be present and paid
Attended Hours =
    SUM ( CrewRoster[AttendedHours] )
Returns
109.0 over six rows. Heavy 68.0, being 40.0, 20.0 and 8.0 across the three months. Light 16.0, cabin and IFE 15.0, line 10.0.
Why SUM
The roster already holds hours. Counting technicians and multiplying by a shift length would have to model part shifts, overtime and absence before it agreed with 109.0, and every one of those assumptions would be yours to defend.
Watch the word
Attended hours are paid presence. All 109.0 of them are on the payroll and only some of them reach a job card. The distance between those two statements is the next measure.

The ceiling: attended hours multiplied by the productivity factor for that service line.

DAXAchievable Hours
// attended times factor, the real ceiling.  87.72
Achievable Hours =
    SUMX (
        CrewRoster,
        CrewRoster[AttendedHours]
            * RELATED ( ProductivityFactor[Factor] )
    )
Lines 3 to 7
SUMX walks CrewRoster one row at a time. RELATED reads that row's factor from ProductivityFactor, which sits on the one side of a many to one relationship on the service line.
Returns
87.72. Row by row, heavy January is 40.0 times 0.88, which is 35.20. Light January is 16.0 times 0.68, which is 10.88. Heavy February is 20.0 times 0.88, which is 17.60. Cabin February is 15.0 times 0.68, which is 10.20. Line March is 10.0 times 0.68, which is 6.80. Heavy March is 8.0 times 0.88, which is 7.04.
Why SUMX
The factor changes with the service line, so the multiplication belongs inside the row. Summing attended hours first and multiplying once picks a single factor for all 109.0 hours, and no such factor exists in this shop.
Why RELATED
A relationship already runs from ProductivityFactor down to CrewRoster, and RELATED follows it from the many side. LOOKUPVALUE would rescan the factor table once per roster row to read the same four values.

The weighted ratio of those two, which is the number a planning pack gets wrong most often.

DAXOverall Productivity Factor
// weighted.  87.72 over 109.0 is 80.5 percent
Overall Productivity Factor =
    DIVIDE ( [Achievable Hours], [Attended Hours] )
Returns
87.72 divided by 109.0 is 0.80477, so 80.5 percent. The same two measures give the ratio for any service line, any month, or the whole quarter, because both halves are recomputed at whatever level the visual asks for.
The trap
Put the factor on the roster as a calculated column and average it and you get 4.68 divided by 6, which is 0.78, so 78.0 percent. Every row gets one vote. Heavy carries 68.0 of the 109.0 attended hours and the cabin row carries 15.0, so one vote each is the wrong arithmetic. This is the average of averages error and it is the most common wrong number in a planning pack.
Why DIVIDE
The slash operator would error on any service line with no roster hours at all, and that error travels up into every subtotal above it. DIVIDE returns blank and the visual keeps working.
Earlier name
Chapter C calls this Productivity Factor and writes both halves out as SUMX expressions. It is the same 80.5 percent. The library puts Overall in the name so that nobody reads it as Performance Factor above, which divides available hours by achieved hours and answers a question about a check rather than about a roster.

Achieved hours are booked time from the timesheet, and that measure is already in this library under heavy maintenance planning, where Achieved Hours reads [Labor Hours] and returns 62.0. The planning chain uses it unchanged. Across the six planning rows it reads heavy January 26.0, light January 9.0, heavy February 12.0, cabin February 10.0, line March 5.0 and heavy March 0.0. Those six add to 62.0, which ties back to every other total in this chapter.

The other achieved number, and the one the business actually reports.

The gap between the two achieved numbers, published as a number of its own.

Scope
This belongs on the data quality page beside Orphan Parts Cost. One hour is small, and it is the entire distance between two numbers that both get quoted as the achieved figure for January.

The last pair splits a missed target into the two things that cause it.

DAXStructural Shortfall, Execution Shortfall
// the roster was too small for the demand
Structural Shortfall =
    [Demand Hours] - [Achievable Hours]

// the crew did not convert the ceiling it was given
Execution Shortfall =
    [Achievable Hours] - [Achieved Hours]
Returns, cabin February
Demand 11.0, achievable 10.20, achieved 10.00. Structural shortfall is 11.0 less 10.20, which is 0.80. Execution shortfall is 10.20 less 10.00, which is 0.20. The two add to the 1.00 hour the line missed against demand, and four fifths of that miss was a rostering decision taken in January.
Returns, quarter
Structural shortfall reads 75.0 less 87.72, which is minus 12.72. Execution shortfall reads 87.72 less 62.0, which is 25.72. A negative structural shortfall means the roster gave more ceiling than the demand needed, which it did on five of the six rows.
Cost
A grand total nets the rows against each other, so the 0.80 that matters disappears into the minus 12.72. Put this pair on a matrix by service line and month. On a card it says nothing useful.
Why two measures
A single variance cannot say which of the two happened, and the two owners are different people. Resource scheduling owns the roster size. The shift supervisor owns the conversion. Give a meeting one number and it will pick an owner by seniority.
Cost again
Both measures stand on the factor, so both are worth exactly what the factor is worth. If 0.68 was set three years ago and nobody has looked at it since, structural shortfall is measuring an assumption and calling it a shortfall.
Common mistake

Averaging the productivity factor. It is the shortest route to a wrong number in this whole chapter, and it survives review because 78.0 percent and 80.5 percent both look like reasonable answers. Weight it by attended hours every time: divide 87.72 by 109.0, once, at whatever level the visual asks for. That rule already governs cost per hour and utilisation earlier in this library, and it will govern the next ratio anybody asks you for.

The planning chain traced by hand, all six rows

Six rows carry every number in this section. Read each one across: demand is what was asked for, attended is what was rostered, achievable is attended times the factor, achieved is what the timesheet recorded, and submitted is what the department reported at the month end.

  • Heavy, January. Demand 30.0, attended 40.0, factor 0.88, achievable 35.20, achieved 26.0, submitted 27.0. The one row in the quarter where the two achieved numbers disagree.
  • Light, January. Demand 10.0, attended 16.0, factor 0.68, achievable 10.88, achieved 9.0, submitted 9.0.
  • Heavy, February. Demand 14.0, attended 20.0, factor 0.88, achievable 17.60, achieved 12.0, submitted 12.0.
  • Cabin and IFE, February. Demand 11.0, attended 15.0, factor 0.68, achievable 10.20, achieved 10.0, submitted 10.0. The only row where demand sits above achievable.
  • Line, March. Demand 6.0, attended 10.0, factor 0.68, achievable 6.80, achieved 5.0, submitted 5.0.
  • Heavy, March. Demand 4.0, attended 8.0, factor 0.88, achievable 7.04, achieved 0.0, submitted 0.0.

Add the columns and you have the five totals the measures return: demand 75.0, attended 109.0, achievable 87.72, achieved 62.0, submitted 63.0. Three headline ratios fall straight out of them. Demand over achievable is 75.0 divided by 87.72, which is 85.5 percent, and it answers whether there was enough capacity for the ask. Achieved over achievable is 62.0 divided by 87.72, which is 70.7 percent, and it answers whether the capacity was used. Achieved over demand is 62.0 divided by 75.0, which is 82.7 percent, and it answers whether the shop delivered what was asked.

Run the cabin row backwards and the rostering decision turns into a number a planner can act on. Cabin and IFE was asked for 11.0 hours at a factor of 0.68, so the roster had to supply 11.0 divided by 0.68, which is 16.18 attended hours. It supplied 15.0. The line was 1.18 attended hours short before anybody picked up a tool, and 1.18 multiplied by 0.68 brings you back to the 0.80 of structural shortfall the measure reported. Same fact, stated in attended hours instead of achievable hours. Every other row in the quarter was covered: heavy January needed 34.09 against 40.0 rostered, light January 14.71 against 16.0, heavy February 15.91 against 20.0, line March 8.82 against 10.0, and heavy March 4.55 against 8.0.

Many to many and the bridge

The resourcing manager wants to know how much A&P work the shop did this quarter. You put certification down the rows and labour hours in the values. The four certification rows add to 85.0. The total row underneath them reads 62.0. He asks which figure he should quote, and both of them are correct.

Certifications are held by technicians, and technicians book hours. A bridge table is what sits between the two: one row per technician per certification held, which is BridgeTechCert in this model. Nothing in the data says which certification was actually used on which job, so any attribution of hours to a certification is an assumption. The first measure below makes that assumption visible. The second counts people instead of hours, which is usually the honest answer. The third allocates, which makes totals reconcile at the cost of inventing a fraction that no timesheet supports.

Hours belonging to technicians who hold the selected certification.

DAXHours With Certification
Hours With Certification =
    CALCULATE (
        [Labor Hours],
        CROSSFILTER (
            DimTechnician[TechnicianID],
            BridgeTechCert[TechnicianID],
            BOTH
        )
    )
Returns
A&P 39.0, Avionics 10.5, NDT 12.0, Engine Run 23.5. Those four add to 85.0 against a true total of 62.0.
Earlier name
Chapter 8 and Chapter 12 call this measure Hours by Certification and define it identically. Chapter 14 calls the same expression Hours Covered Scoped, to sit beside the two other implementations it compares there. The library renames it once more only so that it pairs with Technicians With Certification below. All three are one measure: A&P 39.0, Avionics 10.5, NDT 12.0, Engine Run 23.5, and 62.0 on a total row. Chapter 14 and Chapter 15 also give TREATAS forms that need no bidirectional hop at all and return the same four row values. Their totals are the thing to watch. Hours Covered Virtual in Chapter 14 restricts the total to certified staff and prints 52.0. The Chapter 15 version wraps the traversal in ISFILTERED, so its total comes back to 62.0. Both chapters say which they are doing and why.
Choice
CROSSFILTER inside the measure here. Set the relationship to both directions in the model and the double counting spreads across the whole file. Written into the measure, it exists only where a certification is on the axis, and every other measure keeps a single unambiguous filter path.

Technicians holding the selected certification, which is additive in a way hours are not.

DAXTechnicians With Certification
Technicians With Certification =
    DISTINCTCOUNT ( BridgeTechCert[TechnicianID] )
Returns
A&P 3, Avionics 3, NDT 2, Engine Run 2, and 6 with no certification selected. The row values still add to more than 6, because the same people are counted under several certifications, but nobody reads a headcount as a sum without checking.

The overlap, stated as a number so the reader knows how much of the difference is real.

DAXCertification Overlap Hours
Certification Overlap Hours =
    VAR AcrossCerts =
        SUMX (
            VALUES ( DimCertification[CertCode] ),
            [Hours With Certification]
        )
    VAR Covered = [Hours With Certification]
    RETURN
        AcrossCerts - Covered
Returns
85.0 less 62.0, so 23.0 hours of overlap. That figure is the entire distance between a bridge table and a lie, and it is the number to hand the resourcing manager alongside the 85.0.
Choice
VALUES inside the iterator here, so that the overlap responds to a certification slicer. ALL would ignore the slicer entirely. Pick two certifications and the measure reports the overlap between those two, which is the question a resourcing manager actually asks.
Choice
The subtrahend is [Hours With Certification]. Using [Labor Hours] there looks identical at first, because both read 62.0 on an unfiltered page, and the difference stays invisible until somebody uses the slicer the line above promises. Select A&P and NDT: the iterator adds 39.0 and 12.0 to reach 51.0, and the honest comparison is the 39.0 hours belonging to the four technicians who hold either certification, which leaves 12.0 of overlap. Subtracting a flat 62.0 there would return minus 11.0, and a negative overlap is not a number anyone can act on.
The allocation variant, and the arithmetic that makes it reconcile

Allocation divides each technician's hours by the number of certifications they hold, then gives each certification its share. Totals reconcile. The individual numbers describe nothing that happened.

DAXAllocated Hours
Allocated Hours =
    SUMX (
        CALCULATETABLE (
            VALUES ( DimTechnician[TechnicianID] ),
            CROSSFILTER (
                DimTechnician[TechnicianID],
                BridgeTechCert[TechnicianID],
                BOTH
            )
        ),
        VAR CertsHeld =
            CALCULATE (
                COUNTROWS ( BridgeTechCert ),
                REMOVEFILTERS ( DimCertification )
            )
        VAR CertsInScope =
            CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( BridgeTechCert ) )
        VAR HoursForTech = [Labor Hours]
        RETURN
            DIVIDE ( HoursForTech * CertsInScope, CertsHeld )
    )
Mechanism
The iterator moves one technician at a time, so [Labor Hours] is that technician's hours through context transition. CertsHeld ignores the certification filter and counts everything the person holds. CertsInScope counts only the certifications on the axis, which is 1 in a normal matrix row.
Returns
A&P 26.0, Avionics 3.5, NDT 6.0, Engine Run 16.5. Those add to 52.0, which is the total for known technicians.
Earlier form
Chapter 14 calls this Labor Hours Allocated and iterates BridgeTechCert instead of the technician list, with DIVIDE ( TechHours, CertCount ) and no CertsInScope factor. It returns the same four figures and the same 52.0. Chapter 14 also warns that swapping its bridge iterator for a technician iterator drops the total to 39.0, and that warning applies to the expression written there. This one behaves differently, because CertsInScope puts the multiplier back, so a technician holding three certifications contributes all three thirds on a total row instead of one.

Read the last row carefully before you ship this. The allocated total is 52.0 and the real total is 62.0, because the ten hours booked by the unknown technician TECH-07 have no certifications to be divided between. Allocation always leaks somewhere. Here it leaks exactly where the data quality problem is, which is the one place a leak is tolerable.

Percent of total variants

The finance manager asks for MSP's share of labour hours. Three percent of total measures are already on the page. One reads 55.6%, one reads 60.5%, and the third moves the moment he touches the station slicer. He wants to know which one is right. All three are.

Four measures, four different denominators, all built on Labor Hours. Choosing the wrong one is the most common way a correct model produces a wrong report, because every version returns a plausible percentage. Name the denominator in the measure name, and put that name on the visual. Then the answer to his question is whichever measure divides by the thing he meant.

Share of everything in the model, regardless of any filter on the page.

Choice
ALLSELECTED(DimStation) here, with the table named. Put months down the rows and stations across the columns. The scoped form releases only the station grouping, so each month row adds to 100 percent. A bare ALLSELECTED() releases the month grouping too, so every cell becomes a share of the whole matrix and only the grand total reaches 100.

Share of the level above, for a matrix with more than one field on the rows.

DAXPct of Parent
Pct of Parent =
    VAR Numerator = [Labor Hours]
    VAR Denominator =
        SWITCH (
            TRUE (),
            ISINSCOPE ( DimTechnician[TechnicianName] ),
                CALCULATE (
                    [Labor Hours],
                    REMOVEFILTERS ( DimTechnician )
                ),
            ISINSCOPE ( DimStation[StationCode] ),
                CALCULATE (
                    [Labor Hours],
                    REMOVEFILTERS ( DimStation )
                ),
            [Labor Hours]
        )
    RETURN
        DIVIDE ( Numerator, Denominator )
Returns
On a station then technician matrix, TECH-02 inside MSP reads 16.5 over 34.5. MSP itself reads 34.5 over 62.0. The grand total reads 100 percent.
Choice
ISINSCOPE rather than HASONEVALUE. HASONEVALUE is true whenever a column happens to be filtered to one member, including by a slicer, so a single station selection would make the measure think it is on the technician level. ISINSCOPE tests grouping, which is the actual question.
Order
The SWITCH conditions run deepest level first. Reverse them and every technician row is divided by the station total's parent instead of by the station.

Data quality measures

These belong on a page that only you and the data owner look at. They exist so that a broken source shows up as a number on a screen, weeks before somebody discovers it in a board meeting. Three of them count keys that failed to match, and in a model whose sources agree all three read zero: hours booked to an unknown technician, work orders with no station, and orphan parts lines. None of the three reads zero here. The other two count dimension members that have no facts. Why this and not that

Almost every measure above uses CALCULATE rather than a plain aggregation with a visual level filter. The reason is portability. A visual level filter lives in one visual on one page, cannot be tested or reused, and disappears the moment somebody copies the chart onto a new page. The filter written inside the measure travels with the measure, appears in the definition when a reviewer reads it, and behaves identically in a card, a matrix and an export. The cost is that the filter no longer shows up in the filter pane. A reader who wonders why Closed Work Orders shows 6 while Work Order Count shows 8 has to open the measure to find out. Name measures so that the question does not arise.

Filter modifier quick reference

Most of these are arguments you pass to CALCULATE to change the filter context instead of adding to it. The last three are table functions and a level test that turn up in the same measures, and the table says where each one belongs. The trap column is the failure each one causes most often in a real report.

Interview-level insight

Asked to explain the difference between ALL and ALLSELECTED, the stock answer is that one ignores filters and the other respects them. That is close enough to be wrong. Both remove filters. The difference is where they get the replacement from: ALL replaces the filter with nothing, and ALLSELECTED replaces it with the filter state that existed before the visual added its own row and column grouping. That is why ALLSELECTED gives 62.0 as a denominator with no slicers applied and 35.0 when January is selected, while ALL gives 62.0 in both cases.

Cardinality and direction quick reference

Six settings, and the whole model is built from them. Every one of the sixteen Meridian relationships is many to one and single direction. Two of them, the PromisedDate and ClosedDate date roles, are switched off, and no relationship anywhere in the model filters in both directions.

Fifteen point measure review

The model level review is in Chapter 20. This one is for the DAX. Run it on every measure before it goes into a report that someone else will read. Run it again on any measure that produced a number a stakeholder questioned. A measure that has been questioned once will be questioned again.

  1. Does the name say which date role and which denominator it uses? Work Orders Closed and Work Orders Opened are two measures, never one measure and a bookmark.
  2. Does it return the answer key figure with no filters at all? If Labor Hours is not 62.0 on an empty page, nothing downstream is worth checking.
  3. Does the total equal the sum of the rows, and if not, do you know precisely why? Certification rows adding to 85.0 against a total of 62.0 is acceptable only when it is labelled.
  4. Does every division use DIVIDE? Search the definition for a slash character.
  5. Does every ratio divide a sum by a sum, rather than averaging a per row ratio?
  6. Is every filter modifier scoped to a named table or column? A bare REMOVEFILTERS() should appear only in a measure whose name contains the words all or model wide.
  7. Does the measure survive a date slicer? Set the page to February and check that the number moves in the direction you expect, and by how much.
  8. Does it survive a station slicer, a technician slicer, and both at once?
  9. Does it behave in a card as well as in a table? A card evaluates once, at the top, with no grouping to lean on.
  10. Does it behave at a matrix subtotal? Add the children by hand for one group and compare.
  11. Are there any hidden iterators over a whole fact table that could be a single column predicate instead?
  12. Does it reference a calculated column that could have been made in Power Query? If so, note the refresh cost and decide deliberately.
  13. Does it handle blank inputs the way the audience needs? Blank and zero mean different things, and WO-1008 with no activity is not the same as a work order with zero cost.
  14. Is the format string set on the measure itself, so that every visual inherits it? A percentage that only looks like a percentage in one chart will be exported as a raw decimal.
  15. Can you explain the measure out loud, in one sentence, without reading the code? If not, split it into two measures and let one call the other.
Rule of thumb

Point two of that list catches more defects than the other fourteen combined. Before you write a single measure against a new model, build a table with the four base aggregates and no slicers, and compare it against figures you calculated by hand from the source. It takes ten minutes. Skipping it means every later number you check has two possible explanations, and you will spend a day telling them apart.

What to do next

Rebuild the Meridian model from the six raw tables without looking at the answers. Compare every figure against the key in one table. Pay attention to which ones you got wrong, and let the count of them go, because the pattern is what teaches you something. Write the grain of each table into its description field in model view, in the form one row per technician per work order per day. Grain is the one fact about a table that everybody forgets and nobody can reconstruct from a screenshot. Then do the same on a model of your own, with your own data, where you cannot check the answer at the back of the book, and where the missing station code belongs to a person you can go and talk to. This document has covered the modelling core: grain, keys, relationships, filter context, role playing dimensions, many to many, and the diagnosis of each of those when it goes wrong. It has not covered composite models and the mixed storage modes that come with them, user defined aggregations over a large fact, incremental refresh policies and partitioning, row level security beyond the shape of the problem, or calculation groups past the brief treatment in Chapter 13. Those five sit on top of what you have just learned, and each one is a poor idea in a model whose grain and keys are not already correct. When you meet them, you will meet them because a model that works has become too slow or too large, which is a better problem than the one you started with.

What to remember
  • Diagnose in the order symptom, cause, check, fix. The check column exists to rule causes out, and applying a fix first destroys the evidence you would need if it was the wrong fix.
  • Every measure in the library is a base aggregate plus a filter change. If you know what Labor Hours returns and what the modifier does, you can predict the result before you run it.
  • Name measures after their denominator and their date role. Pct of All Hours and Pct of Visible Hours differ by one modifier, agree on an unfiltered page, and disagree the moment anybody touches a slicer.
  • Many to one, single direction is the default. Bidirectional filtering belongs in one measure, through CROSSFILTER. Set it in the model and it reaches visuals you have not built yet.
  • 62.0 hours, 6,326.50 labour cost, 28,245 parts, 176 planned hours, 8 work orders. Check these four aggregates and the count against a new build before you write anything else.
  • Utilisation at day grain is not available in this data, and no DAX will create it. Say so in the visual title. A measure that stays silent is making a promise the data cannot keep.
  • Demand 75.0, attended 109.0, achievable 87.72, achieved 62.0, submitted 63.0. Five words, five numbers, and the productivity factor is the only bridge between attended and achievable. Weight that factor by attended hours or it reads 78.0 percent instead of 80.5.
