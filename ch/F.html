Appendix

FAviation MRO vocabulary for the data analyst

Every term you will meet in an aircraft maintenance dataset, expanded in full, with the Meridian table or column that carries it and a plain statement where the dataset has nothing at all.

The production controller forwards you a spreadsheet at nine in the morning. One column is headed NRC. Another says FH. A third says Rev. He wants non-routine ratio by month, on a chart, by Thursday. You have never seen two of those three headings before, and by lunchtime you have to tell him whether Thursday is realistic. This appendix is where you look the words up, and by the end of it you will also know why the answer to that particular request is no.

Use it as a reference. Read it once end to end so the shape of the industry lands, then come back to it every time a column name arrives that you cannot decode. The third column of each table is the reason this appendix exists. A dictionary gives you the meaning of a word. This gives you the meaning plus one operational fact: that non-routine ratio is a headline metric at every MRO (maintenance, repair and overhaul shop) in the world, and that you cannot compute it from the eight labour rows you were given. Where a Meridian table carries the concept, it is named. Where nothing carries it, that is said outright, because a gap you know about is a modelling decision and a gap you do not know about is a wrong number on a dashboard.

Two habits will save you more time than anything in this appendix. Expand every acronym the first time it appears in your own documentation, even the ones your colleagues never expand, because the person who inherits your model in two years will not have sat in your meetings. And treat every definition here as the industry norm. Your own shop's definition beats it every time, on every row. Aviation regulation is standardised. Aviation vocabulary is not.

1. Organisations, approvals and roles

You ask the planner why one particular inspection sits on WO-1003. He tells you the customer's programme calls for it. You ask to see the programme, and he tells you it lives in another company's system, in another building, run by people neither of you has met. That sounds like a brush-off. It is the literal truth, and this section is why.

One fact about this industry explains more of its data than any other. The organisation doing the work is usually a different company from the organisation that decided the work was due. That split runs through every table you will touch. It explains orphan keys. It explains why the shop cannot tell you why a task exists. It explains why the customer name is missing on one Meridian work order while the aircraft registration is missing from the fleet list.

Which answers the planner. The CAMO owns the maintenance programme and decides that the inspection is due. Meridian holds the Part-145 approval and performs what it is sent. The reasoning genuinely lives in another company's system, and no amount of digging in the MRO's own data will produce it. Write that sentence down once and you will use it every time somebody asks why a task exists.

2. The maintenance programme

The finance manager looks at a task with an eleven hour estimate on it and asks whether it can come out of this visit to protect the margin. The planner says no, and says it three times, in three different ways, and none of them satisfies anybody in the room. The reason sits four documents upstream of the hangar, and this section walks up that chain.

Nothing in a hangar happens because someone decided it would be a good idea that morning. Every routine task traces back through a chain of approved documents to an agreement between the manufacturer and the regulators. You will rarely see that chain in MRO data. You need to know it exists. It is the reason the shop cannot renegotiate content that looks inefficient, and it is the reason a task with an absurd hours estimate still gets done.

So the planner was right, and he had no way to prove it from anything held in the building. The eleven hour task is in the pack because the operator's AMP calls it up. The AMP inherits it from the MRB report. An AD can force extra content in on top of all that, and an AD is mandatory. None of those documents reaches the shop. What reaches the shop is a package of cards, which is why HeavyPreInput holds hours and holds no reason for any of them.

3. Checks and visits

The finance manager is modelling next year's revenue and asks how often a C-check comes round. You have seen eighteen months written on a slide, so you say eighteen months. The maintenance manager at the end of the table asks which type, which operator, and under whose approved programme. He is right to ask, and the rule at the end of this section is what you should have said instead.

Check letters are the part of aviation vocabulary that outsiders learn first and misuse most. The intervals below vary by aircraft type, operator, programme and authority approval. Treat them as orders of magnitude. Never quote them as facts about a specific fleet, including your own, without asking the planner first.

Rule of thumb

Never quote a check interval as a fact about a fleet. Intervals move with aircraft type, operator, programme and what the authority approved, so every figure in the table above is an order of magnitude and nothing more. When somebody asks how often a C-check falls due on their aircraft, the honest answer is that the planner holds that number and you will go and get it. Ten minutes of asking prevents a slide that a maintenance manager can dismiss in one sentence.

That rule is the sentence the finance manager should have been given. The planner holds the interval for his own fleet, under his operator's approved programme, and fetching it takes ten minutes. Read a figure off a slide instead and the first maintenance manager who sees it will take the whole revenue model down with one question.

4. Manhours and planning

The finance manager wants the labour figure for WO-1003. The planner's pack says 13.0. The production controller's last forecast says 16.5. The timesheet says 12.5. Three people, three numbers, one C-check, and nobody has made a mistake. This section names all three numbers, and WO-1003 is worked through at the end of it.

This group is where careers are made and reports are lost. Three different numbers describe the labour on a single check. Three different functions own them. They live at three different grains. And the words used for them move between shops, and sometimes between two reports inside the same shop. Read every row of this table. Then go and get the local definition in writing.

One warning before you read it. Several words in this group are house vocabulary with no published definition anywhere: attended hours, achievable hours, and available hours in the sense used below, meaning the frozen pre-input baseline for one check. The rows that do rest on a published source name that source, so where a row below names none, treat the term as Meridian's own usage and go and get your own shop's version written down.

Now take WO-1003 back to the finance manager. The planner's 13.0 is available hours, frozen before the aircraft arrived. The production controller's 16.5 is projected hours, Rev 1, raised when findings landed. The timesheet's 12.5 is achieved hours, which is what the technicians booked. All three are correct, all three describe the same C-check, and the two ratios in the table above are built from exactly these figures: performance factor 104.0 percent, forecast accuracy 75.8 percent. Give him one number and he will quote it for a year, so give him all three with their names attached.

Common mistake

Assuming that two columns with the same name mean the same thing. FactPreInput[PlannedHours] is demand: what the work needs. FactCapacity[PlannedHours] is supply: what the shop can provide. They are different facts, at different grains, owned by different people, and both source systems named them correctly from inside their own world. Rename them in the model to Available Hours and Capacity Hours, and put the definition in the description property where a report author will actually see it.

5. Materials and tooling

The shift supervisor rings at four in the afternoon. He has four technicians standing in a bay, the check has stopped moving, and the hours are still being booked to it. Everybody who should be there is there. What is missing is a piece of equipment, and no table in this dataset knows that it exists.

Parts are the second half of a check's cost and often the first cause of its delay. The distinctions below are about how a part is tracked, which is why a hydraulic pump and an in-flight entertainment server sit in the same category despite having nothing in common physically.

Which is the answer to the stalled bay. Three rows above have no table behind them anywhere in Meridian: GSE, special tooling and calibration. A capacity model built on trades alone will report that afternoon as four technicians available, a bay occupied and no reason for anything to be idle. When the shift supervisor tells you the report is wrong, he is describing a gap in the source data, and the honest move is to name that gap on the page.

6. Execution and compliance

The aircraft was finished on Thursday afternoon and it left on Friday morning. The production controller wants to know where that night went, because almost no hours were booked against it. Nothing was broken and nobody was waiting for a part. The answer is in this section, and it is made of paper.

Everything in this group has a physical counterpart in the hangar and a legal counterpart on paper. Treat the paperwork as part of the work. An aircraft with the work finished and the documents incomplete cannot be released, which puts the paper on the critical path and puts it into any honest schedule model.

So the missing night is release paperwork. The work finished, the certifying staff still had cards to sign, and the CRS is what actually ends the visit. Meridian gives you WorkOrders[ClosedDate] and nothing else, so that night shows up as one more day of turnaround time with no labour behind it. Tell the production controller exactly that, and add the sentence to the description of your turnaround measure while you still remember it.

7. Documents and data

An extract lands with a free text column in it, full of strings like AMM 32-41-00 and SRM 53-10-11. Somebody on the call suggests you split that column up and build a dimension out of it. Before you agree, you need to know which half of each string names a manual and which half names a system on the aircraft. This section covers the first half. The table after it covers the second.

Manual references appear constantly in maintenance records, usually as a code in a free text field that nobody planned for you to parse. Nobody expects you to read these manuals. What you need is to recognise the acronyms, so that you can tell a document reference from a part number, and so that you do not build a dimension out of something that is not one.

So the free text column splits two ways. AMM and SRM name manuals, and the digits behind them are ATA chapters. That is enough to answer the suggestion made on the call. One work order normally touches several ATA chapters, which makes chapter a genuine many to many against a work order: one work order reaches several chapters, and one chapter turns up on several work orders. If a findings table ever arrives, model chapter at the finding grain and resist every request to put a single primary chapter on the work order header.

8. Metrics

The finance manager wants utilisation on the wall. The production controller wants turnaround time on the same wall. Both numbers are true, both come out of this quarter's data, and side by side they will start an argument that neither man can win. Put the wall up anyway, and read the callout at the end of this section before the meeting starts.

These are the numbers that appear on the wall. Each of them is defensible, and several of them pull against each other, which is the part nobody puts on the slide. Meridian's quarter is small enough that most of these figures are illustrations, and that is itself a lesson worth carrying.

Interview-level insight

Turnaround time and utilisation pull against each other, and no amount of measure design resolves it. A shop that keeps every technician busy has no slack to recover a check that hits a bad finding, so its turnaround time suffers. A shop that protects turnaround time carries idle capacity and its utilisation looks poor. Meridian shows both ends: 35.2 percent utilisation across the quarter, and still 50.0 percent on-time delivery. If you publish both numbers on one page without acknowledging the tension, you will produce meetings where two teams cite two true figures and reach opposite conclusions.

So both men get their number on the wall, and they get one line of text between them saying that pushing either figure up pushes the other one down. That line is the whole job. Leave it off and 35.2 percent utilisation reads as a shop with nothing to do, while 50.0 percent on-time delivery reads as a shop that cannot plan.

9. Source systems

The planner sends you a fleet list and you find the same physical aircraft in it twice, under two different key formats. He tells you that is impossible, because there is one aircraft and one registration. Both of you are looking at the ordinary consequence of a merger, and this section ends with what to do about it.

The Meridian tables are stand-ins for extracts from real maintenance and engineering systems, usually shortened to M&E. Knowing the product name matters less than knowing one structural fact: the further a table sits from the regulated core, the more likely it is to be a spreadsheet, and the more likely it is to be the table that breaks your model.

One pattern deserves its own paragraph, because it will cost you a day at some point. Larger operators often run more than one M&E system after a merger, and the systems are rarely fully consolidated. The same physical aircraft then appears with two different key formats, one per legacy system, and both keys are genuine. This is the general case of the specific problem Meridian plants in LaborEntries, where TECH-04 and a trailing-space variant of TECH-04 look identical in every table visual and never join. A merger produces the same failure at fleet scale, and no amount of trimming will fix it, because the two keys genuinely differ.

So the planner is looking at two legacy systems and one aeroplane. What you need is a mapping table with one row per aircraft and one column per legacy key format, and somebody in the business has to own it. Ask him who that person is before you leave the conversation. The table itself is easy. The owner is the hard part, and Chapter D is about exactly that.

The vocabulary questions to ask in your first week
  1. For each of available, projected and achieved: which system produces it, who owns that system, and what exactly does the number include? Get the answer in writing and paste it into the model description.
  2. Is performance factor available divided by achieved, or the inverse? Both conventions are in use and the resulting numbers look equally plausible.
  3. Does the frozen baseline ever get re-baselined, and if so, who authorises it and is the original kept? A baseline that quietly moves makes every historical performance number meaningless.
  4. What does the check type column actually contain when the operator runs an equalised programme? Clean A, C and D values are usually somebody's simplification.
  5. Where does inspection, quality and paperwork time get booked? If the answer is nowhere, your utilisation figures are measuring a subset of the working day.
  6. Which functions here are called something else locally? HMCC, MOC and production control are the three most likely to differ, and the function will exist regardless of the label.

How to use this glossary

Start with the request that opened this appendix. NRC means non-routine card, the document raised against a finding. FH means flight hours. Rev is the forecast revision number, and only the latest revision per work order may be used. Non-routine ratio needs a routine flag on every labour booking, and FactLabor carries no such flag, so the chart the production controller wants cannot be built from this data on Thursday or on any other day. What you can give him is the gap between available 57.0 and projected 63.5 hours, plus a written request to the timesheet system owner for that flag.

Then treat every definition above as the industry norm. Your shop's own ruling beats it, every time. When a term appears in a column name, do not look it up here and start modelling. Find out which system produced that column, find out who owns that system, and ask them for the local definition before you write a single line of Power Query. Write their answer down verbatim in the table or column description, with a date and a name against it, because in two years you will not remember whether planned meant the frozen baseline or the live plan. Where they contradict this appendix, they are right and this appendix is general. The one thing you should carry unchanged is the shape: three manhour numbers, three owners, three grains, and a vocabulary that moves when you change buildings.

What to remember
  • The operator's continuing airworthiness function decides what maintenance is due, and the MRO performs it. That split is why so many keys in an MRO dataset point at things the MRO holds no master list for.
  • Available, projected and achieved are three different numbers with three owners and three grains: 57.0, 63.5 and 62.0 hours in this quarter. The words for them are not standardised and will change when you change shops.
  • HMCC, MOC and production control are names for functions. The function exists everywhere. The acronym is local.
  • Expand every acronym in your own documentation, including the ones everybody around you says without thinking. Your successor was not in the room.
  • The most useful entries above are the ones that say the data has no equivalent: non-routine ratio, findings per check, earned hours, schedule adherence and rework. Knowing what you cannot build is what stops you from being asked to build it twice.
