Part 6 . Diagnosis and reference

19Data quality: orphans, blanks and unknown members

After this chapter you can look at a blank row in a visual and name which of nine defects produced it. You can prove which fact rows are responsible. Then you choose between three fixes: recover the key, map it to an explicit unknown member, or send it back to the source system.

The finance manager wants one number for the quarter: what the shop spent on parts. Your summary card says 28,245. The matrix by station on the page behind it adds to 23,345. He asks which one goes in the pack, and the uncomfortable answer is that both of them are right.

Every relationship you create in a Power BI model is an assertion about your data: that every value on the many side exists on the one side. Nothing verifies that assertion for you. The engine accepts the relationship, loads the data, and quietly invents a place to put whatever does not fit. The gap between his two numbers is one of those invented places.

Fact table
A table of events, one row per thing that happened. Meridian has four: Fact FactWorkOrder, FactLabor, FactParts and FactCapacity.
Dimension
A table of the things you group and filter by, one row per thing. Dimension DimAircraft holds one row per aircraft registration.
Foreign key
The column on the fact that names a row in the dimension. FactWorkOrder[ACREG] is the foreign key to DimAircraft[ACREG].
Orphan
A fact row whose foreign key has no matching row in the dimension. WO-1006 is an orphan of DimAircraft.
Blank member
The row Power BI adds to a dimension to hold the orphans. Its key is blank, it has no attributes, and it prints as an empty cell.

What the engine does when a key does not match

Build the relationship DimAircraft[ACREG] to FactWorkOrder[ACREG] and the engine scans the many side for values that have no partner on the one side. Meridian has one: N338WA on WO-1006, which the customer service desk never added to the fleet spreadsheet. The engine accepts all of it. It adds a single blank row to the one side of that relationship and attributes every unmatched fact row to that blank row. The refresh goes green. No warning appears anywhere a report author would see one.

That blank row is virtual. The engine keeps it outside the stored table, so COUNTROWS(DimAircraft) still returns 6. It carries no attribute values, so it renders as an empty cell wherever a visual groups by an aircraft attribute. And there is exactly one of it per relationship. That last part is the one that hurts. A hundred distinct broken aircraft registrations would collapse into the same single empty row, indistinguishable from each other and from a genuinely blank key.

Look at which side of the picture gains a row. On the left, DimAircraft holds the six aircraft registrations the stale registry actually delivered. The seventh box underneath them, drawn with a dashed red outline and labelled (blank), is annotated added by Power BI, not by you, because that is exactly what it is. Follow the dashed red curve back to its other end and it starts on N338WA, the single work order row tinted red on the right, whose 12.0 hours have nowhere else to land. The blank member appears on the one side, the dimension. The bad key itself lives on the fact, one hop away. That is why the defect surfaces as a category with no name instead of a fact row you can click into. Nothing is lost arithmetically. Across this one relationship the rows still sum to the grand total, blank member included, because the unmatched fact row is attributed to the blank member and kept. What the engine loses is the ability to describe it, which is the point the grey card at the foot of the figure makes when it calls the blank row a silent bucket.

Defect 1. The orphan foreign key

The planner wants labor hours by aircraft type for the quarter review. You build the matrix in about a minute. It comes back with four rows, and the fourth row has no name on it at all. That row holds 12.0 hours, and she wants to know whose airframe they went on.

WO-1006 is a scheduled visit on tail N338WA, 12.0 labor hours from TECH-01 and one ENG-8802 engine part at 5,100. The tail is real. The registry is stale. Put aircraft type on the rows of a matrix and the visit moves into a row with no label, and it stays inside the total.

Two defects, two relationships, and only one of them is given a row to sit in. The 12.0 hours and the 5,100 failed the DimAircraft join, so they land on the blank member, which is at least a row a reader can point at. The 4,900 failed a different join, header to line, and it is the next section. That parts line has no work order. With no work order it has no aircraft registration, so it has no aircraft type, and the matrix has nowhere to put it. It appears in the total and in none of the rows. When a visual's total is larger than the sum of the rows printed above it, this is the first thing to test. The difference, here 4,900, is usually the whole of your evidence.

Detection, method one: a left anti join in Power Query

MOrphan aircraft registrations
let
    Source = WorkOrders,
    Unmatched = Table.NestedJoin(
        Source, {"ACREG"},
        AircraftRegistry, {"ACREG"},
        "RegistryHit", JoinKind.LeftAnti
    ),
    Cleaned = Table.RemoveColumns(Unmatched, {"RegistryHit"})
in
    Cleaned
Line 3
Table.NestedJoin with JoinKind.LeftAnti rather than a normal left outer merge followed by a filter on nulls. The anti join returns only the left rows with no partner, so the answer is the row count, and it never materialises the matched side. The outer-merge-then-filter version builds the full joined table first and then throws most of it away.
Line 8
The anti join still adds the nested column, always empty, so drop it. Leaving it in the query costs nothing at refresh but confuses the next person who opens it.
Result
One row, WO-1006. Point a card at the row count of this query and you have a permanent check. Zero is healthy.

Detection, method two: a measure in the model

DAXOrphan Aircraft Rows
Orphan Aircraft Rows =
COUNTROWS (
    FILTER (
        FactWorkOrder,
        ISBLANK ( RELATED ( DimAircraft[ACREG] ) )
    )
)
COUNTROWS
COUNTROWS counts rows of a table, blank keys included. COUNT on a column skips blanks, and blank keys are precisely what you are trying to count, so it would report zero on the worst data you own.
FILTER
FILTER supplies a row context, and this test needs one, because RELATED only means something while the engine is standing on a specific fact row. A boolean predicate inside CALCULATE is evaluated with no row context, so it cannot do this job at all.
RELATED
RELATED walks the relationship that already exists, which is the cheapest route to the dimension. LOOKUPVALUE ignores relationships and performs its own match per row: the same answer at higher cost, and different behaviour if the key is ever duplicated.
ISBLANK
ISBLANK tests for blank and stops there. Write = BLANK() and DAX treats an empty string as equal to blank, so the test would also flag a key that exists but is empty. Those are two different defects with two different owners, and you want them counted separately.
Result
1 on the raw Meridian model. The fact row lands on the virtual blank member, RELATED finds no attribute to return, and the row is counted.

There is a shorter trick. Run it on the raw model, where the aircraft dimension is still the registry as it arrived, 6 rows. COUNTROWS(VALUES(DimAircraft[ACREG])) returns 7 while COUNTROWS(DISTINCT(DimAircraft[ACREG])) returns 6, because VALUES includes the virtual blank row of an invalid relationship and DISTINCT leaves it out. The difference is 1 whenever any orphan exists. The limitation is that it is always 1, however many orphans there are, so it tells you the model is broken without telling you how badly.

The fix

Build DimAircraft from the union of the registry and the distinct aircraft registrations actually used on work orders, exactly as in Chapter 3. That takes the dimension to 7 rows and gives N338WA a home. The 12.0 hours and the 5,100 move out of the blank row into a labelled row that says the type is unknown. The number keeps its value. What changes is its address.

So the planner gets her fourth row back with a name on it. It reads Unknown type, it still holds 12.0 hours, and she can walk to customer service and ask which airframe N338WA is. Somebody can answer that question in a minute. Nobody can answer a blank row.

Rule of thumb

A blank row in a visual is the model reporting a failed assertion in the only vocabulary it has. Read it the way you would read a null in a foreign key column in a database. It is an incident with an owner, and somebody has to go and chase it.

Defect 2. The orphan header key

P-9007 issues two HYD-4471 units at 2,450 each against WO-1009. There is no WO-1009 in WorkOrders. The stores clerk typed a work order number that the shop floor system never created, or created and voided. The parts left the store either way, and the cost is real.

This orphan behaves differently from the first one, because the broken relationship is fact to fact, header to line. Fact FactWorkOrder is the one side. It gains the blank row. So the 4,900 has no work order. With no work order it has no station, no aircraft, no work order type and no opened date. It falls out of every breakdown you can build.

MSP at 8,790 is arithmetic on the source lines: 1,240 plus 2,450 plus 5,100. The rest of the column comes straight from the answer key. Read this table for the shape of the failure. Which station carries which number matters far less than the two rows at the bottom.

What you see in the report

Two visuals on the same page, fed by the same measure, disagreeing by 4,900. The card says 28,245. The matrix by station adds to 23,345. Both are correct, and neither is a bug. The matrix shows every parts line that can be attributed to a station. The card shows every parts line. The gap between those two statements is a data quality defect that neither visual has any way to declare. A finance reviewer will assume the matrix is broken and stop trusting the page.

That answers the finance manager, and he will not enjoy it. Both of his numbers are correct, so neither one can be fixed. What he actually needs is the third number, 4,900, and the sentence that goes with it: two hydraulic units left the store against a work order that no system in the shop can find.

Trace the 4,900 gap by hand, line by line

Seven lines in FactParts. Six carry a WorkOrderID that exists in FactWorkOrder, so each inherits a station through its header.

  • P-9001, WO-1001, 4 times 310 equals 1,240, station MSP.
  • P-9002, WO-1002, 1 times 2,450 equals 2,450, station MSP.
  • P-9003, WO-1003, 2 times 5,100 equals 10,200, station TUS.
  • P-9004, WO-1003, 8 times 310 equals 2,480, station TUS.
  • P-9005, WO-1005, 1 times 1,875 equals 1,875, station blank, mapped to UNASSIGNED.
  • P-9006, WO-1006, 1 times 5,100 equals 5,100, station MSP.
  • P-9007, WO-1009, 2 times 2,450 equals 4,900, no header, so no station at all.

Six attributable lines sum to 23,345. Add P-9007 and you get 28,245. Both visuals compute the same measure over different row sets, and the only thing deciding which row set you get is whether the visual groups by an attribute on the far side of a broken join.

Detection is the same anti join as before, with PartsIssued on the left and WorkOrders on the right, keyed on WorkOrderID. The fix has three options. Two of them tell the reader the truth, and the middle one buys a tidy report by hiding the problem. You can chase WO-1009 in the shop floor system and find out whether it was voided, mistyped, or belongs to a different period. You can create an inferred header row for WO-1009 with a quoted amount of zero and an unknown station, which makes the matrix add up. Or you can leave it visible and labelled. Meridian ships with this one deliberately unfixed so the unknown member lesson has something to catch.

The inferred header carries a price. Total quoted amount is 38,100 and total actual cost is 29,671.50, which is 6,326.50 of labor plus the 23,345 of attributable parts. Add an inferred WO-1009 with quoted zero and total actual cost becomes 6,326.50 plus 28,245, which is 34,571.50, while quoted stays at 38,100. Your quote to actual variance moves by 4,900, because you invented a work order that was quoted at nothing. Fixing the join broke the margin report. That is the trade, and you should make it consciously.

Defect 3. The blank foreign key

WO-1005 has no StationCode. The field is empty. It is an AOG job that someone opened before deciding where the aircraft would be recovered to, and nobody went back to fill the field in.

A blank on the many side behaves exactly like an unmatched key. It is attributed to the blank member on the one side. In a labor hours by station visual the effect is invisible, because WO-1005 has no labor rows. In a parts cost by station visual its 1,875 sits in an empty row beside MSP and TUS, and readers take that row for a station whose name did not print.

Detection: count the blanks before you map them

MBlank station codes
let
    Source = WorkOrders,
    Blanks = Table.SelectRows(
        Source,
        each [StationCode] = null
             or Text.Trim([StationCode]) = ""
    ),
    Count = Table.RowCount(Blanks)
in
    Count
Line 3
Table.SelectRows counts the rows yourself, over the whole table, which is the answer. The Empty percentage on the column quality bar is the faster look, and it samples the first 1,000 rows unless you switch it to the whole data set, so a single bad row in a large table rounds to zero percent and the bar tells you the column is fine. Treat the bar as a prompt and this query as the answer.
Lines 5 and 6
Both tests, for the same reason the mapping step below uses both. A CSV export delivers null and an Excel export delivers an empty string, and a column that arrived from two sources over two quarters holds both. Text.Trim catches the third form, a cell holding nothing but spaces, which passes a null test and an empty string test and still joins to nothing.
Result
1 on the Meridian work orders, WO-1005. Point a card at it and zero is the healthy reading. The DAX equivalent is Blank Station Keys, later in this chapter, which reads 1 on the raw work orders and 0 once the mapping runs, because the key is then the string UNASSIGNED.
Scope
Run the same three lines over FactLabor[TechnicianID], FactParts[WorkOrderID] and every other relationship column before you build the model. Running them after a reader queries a number is running them late. Blank keys are the one defect class you can enumerate completely in advance, because the test does not depend on the other side of the join.

The fix

MMap blank station to an explicit member
let
    Source = WorkOrders,
    Mapped = Table.TransformColumns(
        Source,
        {{"StationCode", each
            if _ = null or Text.Trim(_) = ""
            then "UNASSIGNED"
            else Text.Upper(Text.Trim(_)), type text}}
    )
in
    Mapped
Line 3
Table.TransformColumns takes a function, so one step catches null, empty string and whitespace-only in a single pass. Replace Values from the ribbon generates a step that matches one literal, which means three steps and three chances to forget one.
Line 6
Both tests, because a blank cell in a CSV export arrives as null and a blank cell in an Excel export often arrives as an empty string. The two look the same in the preview pane and behave differently in a join.
Line 8
The normalisation is applied to the good values as well as the bad ones. Cleaning only the failures leaves you with a column that is half normalised, which is how you get a defect that appears one month and not the next.
Pairing
Add a matching UNASSIGNED | Unassigned | n/a row to Built DimStation, taking it to 5 rows. A mapped fact key with no dimension member is still an orphan.

An explicit UNASSIGNED member beats a blank for one reason. A blank is indistinguishable from a join failure, so as long as the row is blank you cannot tell whether the station is genuinely unknown or whether your model is broken.

Defect 4. Two keys that print the same and join differently

L-505 records 4.5 hours from "TECH-04 ". The trailing space is invisible in the query editor preview, in a table visual, in a tooltip and in the filter pane. Drop TechnicianID into a table visual and you get two rows that both read TECH-04. They are two different values. One joins to D. Sandoval, the other joins to nothing.

MMake the invisible visible
let
    Source = LaborEntries,
    Lengths = Table.AddColumn(Source, "KeyLength",
        each Text.Length([TechnicianID]), Int64.Type),
    Codes = Table.AddColumn(Lengths, "CharCodes",
        each Text.Combine(
            List.Transform(
                Text.ToList([TechnicianID]),
                each Text.From(Character.ToNumber(_))
            ), "."), type text)
in
    Codes
KeyLength
Use Text.Length for the test. A trim-and-compare test tells you the value changed, and it stays silent on any character that trim leaves in place, and those are the ones that cost you a week.
CharCodes
The definitive test. Character 32 is an ordinary space. Character 160 is a non breaking space, which is what you get when a key is copied out of a web page or lifted from a PDF, and Text.Trim will not always remove it, because its remit is the standard whitespace set. If you see 160 in this column, replace it explicitly.
In DAX
The same test is a calculated column, LEN(FactLabor[TechnicianID]). Use LEN for detection. DAX TRIM removes character 32 only, so a trim-based test would silently hide the case you most need to see. Delete the column once you have the answer.
MNormalise the key, both sides
let
    Source = LaborEntries,
    Normalised = Table.TransformColumns(
        Source,
        {{"TechnicianID", each
            Text.Upper(
                Text.Trim(
                    Text.Replace(_, Character.FromNumber(160), " ")
                )
            ), type text}}
    )
in
    Normalised
Order
Replace the non breaking space first, then trim, then case. Trimming first leaves a character 160 sitting at the end of the string where trim cannot see it.
Text.Upper
Applied on both the fact and the dimension, always as a pair. Normalising one side and leaving the other converts a matching key into a non matching one.
Effect
L-505 joins, TECH-04 goes from 6.0 hours to 10.5, and the 4.5 hours leave the blank row. Total labor hours stays at 62.0 throughout, which is why nobody noticed.

Defect 5. Case variation, which the two engines disagree about

No row in the Meridian extract carries a case problem today. This one arrives the afternoon somebody tidies a key by hand, or the day a second source starts feeding the same column, and the two tools you use to build the model take opposite views of what happened.

Power Query is case sensitive. TECH-04 and tech-04 are two values to a merge, to Remove Duplicates, and to Table.Group. The DAX engine, as it is used to resolve relationships and to group in a visual, is case insensitive under the default collation. It treats those two strings as one key.

The two behaviours point in opposite directions, and both directions bite.

  • A key that fails a Power Query merge can still match in the model. You spend an afternoon fixing a merge that produced nulls, load the result, and the relationship works fine, which makes you doubt your own diagnosis.
  • A dimension that Power Query considers clean can be rejected by the model. Run Remove Duplicates on a DimTechnician containing both TECH-04 and tech-04 and Power Query keeps both, because to it they are distinct. Then the model refuses to build the relationship, because to it they are one duplicated key.

The fix is to make the difference stop mattering. Normalise case explicitly in Power Query, on both the fact and the dimension, so that neither behaviour is load bearing.

Common mistake

Changing a text key to a numeric type because the profile pane says it is all digits. A technician id of 04 becomes 4, a part sequence of 0812 becomes 812, and the join to a dimension that kept the text form fails on every row with a leading zero. Meridian happens to escape this because every key carries a letter prefix, which forces text detection. That is luck. Nobody designed it. The moment someone strips the TECH- prefix to make a chart look tidier, the defect arrives, and Power Query's automatic Changed Type step will do it without asking.

Defect 6. Duplicate keys on the one side

The refresh failed overnight and the Monday manpower report is empty. The production controller has asked about it twice already. This is the friendliest defect in the chapter, because the refresh history hands you a table name, a column name and the offending value.

This one stops you dead before a blank row is ever drawn. Build DimAircraft by appending AircraftRegistry to the distinct aircraft registrations from WorkOrders, forget the deduplication step, and you have 11 rows for 7 aircraft, because the four tails present in both sources appear twice.

At design time, the relationship dialog refuses with a message along the lines of "You can't create a relationship between these two columns because one of the columns must have unique values". At refresh time, a relationship that was valid and has since gone bad fails the whole refresh with a message naming the offending value: "Column 'ACREG' in Table 'DimAircraft' contains a duplicate value 'N771QR' and this is not allowed for columns on the one side of a many-to-one relationship". The exact wording drifts between versions. The second form is more useful because it names the value, and it is the one you will meet in production at seven in the morning.

MFind the offending keys
let
    Source = DimAircraft,
    Counted = Table.Group(
        Source, {"ACREG"},
        {{"Rows", each Table.RowCount(_), Int64.Type}}
    ),
    Offenders = Table.SelectRows(Counted, each [Rows] > 1)
in
    Offenders
Table.Group
Rather than Remove Duplicates. Remove Duplicates makes the problem go away without telling you which key was duplicated or how many times, and on a real dimension the duplicate is usually the symptom of something upstream that you want to know about.
Line 7
An empty result is the healthy state. Keep this query in the file, disable its load, and check it whenever a refresh fails on a relationship.
Caution
Because this runs in Power Query it is case sensitive, so it will report a clean dimension that the model still rejects. Normalise case before you group, or you are testing a different question from the one the engine asks.

So the production controller gets a straight answer inside five minutes. Four aircraft registrations appear twice in the appended dimension, the deduplication step went missing when somebody reordered the query, and the refresh will run as soon as it goes back in. He can tell the shift that the manpower report will be there by eight.

Unknown members, done properly

Defects 1, 2 and 3 all end at the same fix, so this section builds that fix properly once and the numbered list picks up again afterwards.

An unknown member is a real row in the dimension, with a key you control and descriptive text that states what it means. It exists so that unmatched facts land somewhere labelled instead of nowhere. There are two ways to build one, and swapping one for the other changes what you can find out afterwards.

Mapped unknown
The dimension gets a hard coded row with a fixed key, UNASSIGNED or -1. Power Query rewrites the fact key to that value wherever it is blank or unmatched. The original value, if any, is discarded.
Recovered key, sometimes called an inferred member
The dimension gets one row per unmatched key, carrying the actual key value with unknown attributes filled in. The fact is untouched. Every distinct broken key stays distinguishable.

Variant one: the mapped unknown

Two halves, and both are compulsory. The dimension gets a row you wrote by hand, with a key you chose and text that states what the row means. The fact gets a step that redirects any key that is blank or absent from the dimension onto that chosen key. Ship only the first half and nothing lands on your new row. Ship only the second half and you have converted an orphan into a different orphan.

MUnknown member, dimension side and fact side
// Query 1. DimStation gains the row.
let
    Known = StationsAppended,
    Unknown = #table(
        type table [
            StationCode = text,
            StationName = text,
            StationRole = text
        ],
        {{"UNASSIGNED", "Unassigned",
          "No station recorded on the work order"}}
    ),
    Combined = Table.Combine({Known, Unknown})
in
    Combined

// Query 2. FactWorkOrder is redirected onto it.
let
    Source = WorkOrders,
    Valid = List.Buffer(StationsAppended[StationCode]),
    Mapped = Table.AddColumn(Source, "StationKey",
        each if [StationCode] = null
                or not List.Contains(Valid, [StationCode])
             then "UNASSIGNED"
             else [StationCode], type text),
    Dropped = Table.RemoveColumns(Mapped, {"StationCode"}),
    Renamed = Table.RenameColumns(Dropped,
        {{"StationKey", "StationCode"}})
in
    Renamed
#table
Rather than Enter Data from the ribbon. Enter Data writes the row into the query as a compressed base64 string, which nobody can read, review or diff, and which pins the column types at whatever they were the day you typed it. A #table literal is plain text in the query, so the next person can see the member exists and change its wording without re-entering the data.
The key you choose
A word for text keys, -1 for numeric surrogate keys, because real surrogates are positive. Avoid 0 and avoid an empty string. Both occur naturally in source systems, and the day one arrives for real you will have two meanings sharing one key with no way to separate them.
List.Buffer
Buffer the list of valid codes once, before the each starts running. Reference StationsAppended[StationCode] inside the each and the column is re-evaluated per fact row, so the cost becomes rows times members. On eight work orders that is invisible. On two million rows it is the difference between a refresh and a cancelled refresh.
Both tests
List.Contains catches the unmatched key and the null test catches the missing one, because they are different defects that happen to deserve the same destination here. If you want them separated later, send them to two members.
New column, then drop, then rename
The redirect writes StationKey, the next step removes the original StationCode, and the last step renames StationKey back to StationCode. While you are developing you can see both side by side. The rename matters because the relationship to DimStation and the Blank Station Keys measure later in this chapter both name that column, and a column rename inside Power Query breaks every one of them silently. For Meridian nothing is lost by dropping the original, because the value being replaced is blank.
Result
Built DimStation goes from 4 rows to 5. WO-1005 groups under Unassigned. Labor hours by station stays where it was, because WO-1005 has no labor rows, and parts cost by station moves 1,875 out of the blank row and into a labelled one.

Variant two: the recovered key

MDimAircraft with the recovered key
let
    Registry = AircraftRegistry,
    Tails = Table.Distinct(
        Table.SelectColumns(WorkOrders, {"ACREG"})),
    Missing = Table.NestedJoin(
        Tails, {"ACREG"}, Registry, {"ACREG"},
        "Hit", JoinKind.LeftAnti),
    Keys = Table.SelectColumns(Missing, {"ACREG"}),
    A = Table.AddColumn(Keys, "AircraftType",
        each "Unknown type", type text),
    B = Table.AddColumn(A, "Operator",
        each "Unknown operator", type text),
    C = Table.AddColumn(B, "EngineType",
        each "Unknown engine", type text),
    D = Table.AddColumn(C, "YearBuilt",
        each null, Int64.Type),
    Combined = Table.Combine({Registry, D})
in
    Combined
Lines 5 to 7
The anti join does all the work here. It produces exactly the keys the registry is missing, which for Meridian is N338WA and nothing else. Next quarter it might be three tails, and the query needs no change.
Lines 9 to 16
Literal text in the descriptive columns. A null here would put the row straight back into a blank cell in every visual and you would have gained nothing.
YearBuilt
Left null on purpose, because it is numeric and the text "Unknown" cannot go in a numeric column without changing the type and breaking any measure that averages it. Not every attribute can carry an unknown label, and pretending otherwise is how you get a fleet whose average age is wrong.
Result
7 rows. The relationship becomes valid, the virtual blank member disappears, and WO-1006 groups under "Unknown type" where a human can see it and act.

Which variant to use is decided by one question: is the key itself information? For Meridian's aircraft it is. N338WA is a real aircraft registration, it identifies a specific airframe, and someone in customer service can look it up and tell you who operates it. Recover it. Mapping it to a generic unknown would throw away the one piece of evidence that lets somebody resolve the defect.

For Meridian's station on WO-1005 the answer is the opposite. There is no value to recover. The field was never filled in, so there is nothing to look up, and every future blank belongs in the same bucket as this one. Map it to UNASSIGNED. The general rule: recover when a broken key is a real identifier you failed to resolve, map when the key is genuinely absent.

Defect 7. Type mismatches on the relationship columns

Two columns can hold what looks like the same value and still refuse to join, because the model compares stored values and not printed ones.

  • Text against number. A MonthKey of 202501 stored as whole number in DimDate and as text in FactCapacity. Power BI will refuse to create the relationship at all. This is the benign case, because it fails loudly at design time.
  • Date against date and time. This is the one that hurts. If the timesheet export delivers WorkDate with a time component and DimDate[Date] holds midnight values, the relationship is accepted, because both columns are date based. Then every labor row whose time is not exactly midnight matches nothing. Total labor hours still reads 62.0. Labor hours by month reads zero. Totals agreeing while breakdowns collapse is the signature of this defect.
  • Locale on the type conversion. Meridian's dates arrive ISO formatted, so 2025-01-06 is unambiguous. Change the export to 06/01/2025 and the Changed Type step will read it as 6 January or 1 June depending on the locale of the machine that authored the step, which is not necessarily the locale of the service that refreshes it. Set the locale explicitly in Table.TransformColumnTypes. The default is whatever the authoring machine happened to be set to.

The habit that prevents all three: set the type on every key column deliberately, in a named step near the end of the query, and never rely on the automatic Changed Type that Power Query adds when you first connect. That step is generated from a sample of the first rows and it will change its mind when the data changes.

Defect 8. Attributes that change under you

DimAircraft says N615RB is operated by Northwind Regional. Suppose it transfers to Cascade Air in April. The customer service desk updates the fleet spreadsheet, you refresh, and Q1 rewrites itself. Parts cost for Northwind Regional falls from 14,555, which is 12,680 on N902TT plus 1,875 on N615RB, to 12,680. Cascade Air rises from 3,690, which is 1,240 plus 2,450, to 5,565. Nobody touched a fact row. A number you reported in April is a different number in May.

That is a type 1 slowly changing dimension. The attribute is overwritten and history is restated. It is the default behaviour of every dimension you build by reloading the source, and it is sometimes correct, when you want current attributes applied to all history. A type 2 dimension adds a new row per version, with a surrogate key and validity dates, and the fact stores the surrogate that was current when the work order opened.

The cost is real. Type 2 means ACREG can no longer be the relationship key, because it is no longer unique. Every fact load has to resolve the correct version by date before it lands, which Power Query does badly and slowly. For a shop this size the honest answer is usually to stay on type 1 and document that operator describes the aircraft today, and that Q1 restates whenever an aircraft changes hands.

Defect 9. The key your own mapping table has not heard of

The production controller wants to know which crews delivered the quarter. You build him a bar chart of labor hours by service line, routed through the technician who booked each hour. Four bars carry a name. The fifth bar carries 10.0 hours and no name at all. He points at it and asks what that one is, and you do not have an answer for him yet.

Three terms before the mechanism, because this defect lives in the space between them. A service line is one of the four ways Meridian groups its work: Heavy, Light, Line, and Cabin and IFE. A crew code is the roster system's identifier for a crew, such as CRW-AF1 for Airframe Heavy. Built CrewCodeMap is the six row table that turns a crew code into a service line, and no system in the shop produces it. You typed it.

Labor hours reach a service line in three hops. FactLabor carries a technician id. CrewAssignment says which crew that technician belongs to. CrewCodeMap says which service line that crew works for. Two of those hops run on tables a real system maintains and refreshes. The third runs on yours.

TECH-07 booked 10.0 hours to WO-1004 in February. The roster system has TECH-07 on crew CRW-XX9, and the roster system is right. CRW-XX9 is a live crew code that the resource scheduler opened and never thought to mention to you. CrewCodeMap holds CRW-AF1, CRW-AV1, CRW-CAB, CRW-LN1, CRW-LT1 and CRW-LT2. The seventh code is missing from it, the lookup returns nothing, and 10.0 of the quarter's 62.0 hours land in a service line with no name.

Read the last two rows together, because they fail in opposite directions. Light shows 0.0 hours under a name, and that is a true statement about the shop. L. Petrov is on crew CRW-LT1, the crew is mapped, and he booked nothing in Q1. The blank row shows 10.0 hours under no name, and that is a statement about your model. Nothing on the chart separates the two, and the controller has no way to tell a quiet crew from a hole in your mapping.

Why this class is worse than an ordinary orphan

N338WA in defect 1 is somebody else's problem as well as yours. The customer service desk owns the fleet spreadsheet, the tail is missing from it, and an email can end with the registry gaining a row. CRW-XX9 has no route out like that. Four things make it a harder class to live with.

  • No upstream system will ever fix it. The roster system issued CRW-XX9 correctly and holds no opinion about service lines. It has never heard of CrewCodeMap and it never will. The crew code is valid in every system that stores it, and the only place it fails is a table that exists inside your report file.
  • Nothing raises an error. The refresh is green. No relationship is invalid, no key is duplicated, no type is wrong. A left outer merge that finds no match returns null, which in Power Query is an ordinary answer to an ordinary question, and null is what arrives on the report as a blank bar.
  • The total still reconciles. The crew route adds to 62.0 with the blank row included, and so does the work order route. Any reconciliation you built to catch this passes, because the defect moves hours between rows and never loses one.
  • You are the only person who can notice. Every other defect in this chapter has a second pair of eyes somewhere: a stores clerk, a planner, a refresh log, a failed relationship dialog. This one has you. And it grows, because every crew code the scheduler opens from now on lands in the same unnamed bar until somebody tells you it exists.

Detection: count the codes, then price them

Two numbers, and you need both. The count says whether the map is complete. The hours say whether the gap matters. One unmapped code covering a technician who books nothing is a note for the backlog. One covering 10.0 hours a quarter is a hole in the manpower report.

DAXUnmapped Crew Codes
Unmapped Crew Codes =
COALESCE (
    COUNTROWS (
        EXCEPT (
            DISTINCT ( CrewAssignment[CrewCode] ),
            DISTINCT ( CrewCodeMap[CrewCode] )
        )
    ),
    0
)
EXCEPT
The same choice, and the same reason, as Parts Rows With No Header later in this chapter. EXCEPT returns the rows of the first table that are absent from the second in one set operation, and it keeps the lineage of the first table. The alternative, FILTER over CrewAssignment with NOT CONTAINS, performs a lookup for every candidate row and gives you a table with no lineage to reuse.
DISTINCT
DISTINCT here, for the reason set out in the why box further down. If you ever relate CrewCodeMap to CrewAssignment, VALUES on the mapped side starts returning the virtual blank member, that blank joins the set of valid codes, and the check quietly certifies the defect as clean. A measure whose job is finding broken keys must never call the function that hides them.
COALESCE
COUNTROWS over an empty table returns blank, so a healthy model would show an empty card and look like a measure somebody broke. COALESCE states the fallback once. The IF ( ISBLANK ( ... ) , 0, ... ) form writes the whole expression twice to say the same thing.
Reading
1 on Meridian, and the code behind it is CRW-XX9. Swap COUNTROWS for CONCATENATEX over the same EXCEPT and the card prints the code itself, which is the difference between knowing something is wrong and knowing who to ask.
DAXHours On Unmapped Crews
Hours On Unmapped Crews =
VAR MissingCodes =
    EXCEPT (
        DISTINCT ( CrewAssignment[CrewCode] ),
        DISTINCT ( CrewCodeMap[CrewCode] )
    )
VAR MissingTechs =
    CALCULATETABLE (
        DISTINCT ( CrewAssignment[TechnicianID] ),
        MissingCodes
    )
RETURN
    CALCULATE (
        SUM ( FactLabor[Hours] ),
        TREATAS ( MissingTechs, FactLabor[TechnicianID] )
    )
CALCULATETABLE
MissingCodes carries lineage on CrewAssignment[CrewCode], so handing it to CALCULATETABLE filters CrewAssignment directly, with no relationship involved, before DISTINCT is evaluated. FILTER would iterate the assignment table and test each row against the list.
TREATAS
There is no relationship from CrewAssignment to FactLabor, and you should not create one. TREATAS stamps the lineage of FactLabor[TechnicianID] onto the list of technicians for the life of this one calculation and leaves the model alone.
SUM
The measure adds one stored column with no per row expression to evaluate, so SUM is resolved entirely by the storage engine. SUMX would open a row context that nothing in this measure needs.
Reading
10.0 on Meridian, which is L-506 and nothing else.
Limitation
This counts hours booked by technicians on an unmapped crew. It matches the blank row on the report only while the technician key itself is clean. Leave the trailing space on "TECH-04 " from defect 4 in place and 4.5 more hours sit in the blank bar that this measure will never see, because that row fails one hop earlier.
Why this and not that

Use TREATAS here and leave the model alone. A new relationship from CrewAssignment to FactLabor looks tidier and would let the measure use a plain CALCULATE. It also creates a second filter path from the technician to the labor rows, one through DimTechnician and one through CrewAssignment, which is the ambiguity the model already avoids on DimStation to DimTechnician[HomeStation]. Adding a permanent relationship to serve one diagnostic measure is the wrong trade. TREATAS buys the same filter for the duration of one evaluation and disappears afterwards.

Run the same set difference with the two sides swapped and you get the opposite finding, crew codes you have mapped that nobody is rostered to, which today returns CRW-LT2. That one is tidiness and not correctness, so review it once a quarter instead of watching it daily.

The fix: an explicit Unmapped member

You cannot invent the service line for CRW-XX9, and guessing it would be worse than leaving it. What you can do is stop the gap being invisible. Give the missing case a name, write that name into the model at refresh, and the chart shows a bar labelled Unmapped where it used to show a bar labelled nothing.

MCrew to service line, with the gap given a name
let
    Source = CrewAssignment,
    Merged = Table.NestedJoin(
        Source, {"CrewCode"},
        CrewCodeMap, {"CrewCode"},
        "MapRows", JoinKind.LeftOuter
    ),
    Expanded = Table.ExpandTableColumn(
        Merged, "MapRows",
        {"ServiceLine"}, {"ServiceLine"}
    ),
    Named = Table.TransformColumns(
        Expanded,
        {{"ServiceLine", each
            if _ = null then "Unmapped"
            else _, type text}}
    )
in
    Named
JoinKind.LeftOuter
This step has to carry all seven technicians forward with a service line attached, so it keeps everything and labels the failure. JoinKind.LeftAnti, the kind used for detection higher up this chapter, answers "which codes are missing" and throws away the six that matched.
Table.ExpandTableColumn
Expand the nested column at refresh instead of leaving it and reaching into it from DAX. A plain text column is something a slicer, a legend, a matrix row header and a report level filter can all use. A nested table column is none of those things.
The literal Unmapped
A word a reader can see. A null and an empty string both render as the same blank cell you are trying to leave behind. Unmapped is a value a reader can see on an axis, click on, filter to, and complain about, and complaints are how this defect gets closed.
Both halves
The query runs over CrewAssignment, which holds one row per technician, so its output merges straight onto Dimension DimTechnician and the service line becomes a plain text attribute there. Nothing else is needed. If you instead relate that column to Built DimServiceLine, that dimension needs a matching Unmapped row of its own, exactly like the UNASSIGNED pairing in the unknown member section. A mapped fact key with no dimension member is still an orphan.
Result
The seventh technician carries the text Unmapped. The chart gains a labelled bar holding 10.0 hours, the crew route still totals 62.0, and the work order route is untouched. The number keeps its value. What changes is its address.

Now go back to the production controller. The fifth bar says Unmapped, it still holds 10.0 hours, and you can tell him what it means: those hours were booked by a technician on crew CRW-XX9, a crew the roster system opened and your service line map has never been told about. That is a sentence he can act on. He knows the resource scheduler, and one message closes it.

A bar labelled Unmapped on a page a customer sees is embarrassing, and the embarrassment is the point. A blank bar gets scrolled past. A bar with that word on it gets asked about, and the question travels to the one person who can answer it. Until somebody does, the report is at least telling the truth about how much it knows.

Common mistake

Reading a green refresh as evidence that the mappings are complete. A refresh succeeds when every query returns a table, and a left outer merge that matched nothing returns a table full of nulls. Delete all six rows from CrewCodeMap and Meridian still refreshes green, the crew route still totals 62.0, and every named service line reports zero while the blank reports the whole quarter. The only thing standing between you and that report is a check you wrote yourself.

Keep Unmapped Crew Codes and Hours On Unmapped Crews next to the measure set in the following section, on the same hidden diagnostics page, and leave them out of the Model Health Issues total. A count of codes and a count of orphan fact rows are different units, and adding them produces a number that rises for two unrelated reasons. Two cards side by side, reading 1 and 10.0 today, and 0 and 0.0 on a day when somebody has been paying attention.

A data quality measure set you can paste into any model

You have found five live defects by hand in this chapter: N338WA, WO-1009, the blank station on WO-1005, the trailing space on "TECH-04 " and CRW-XX9. Next quarter there will be a sixth, and the planner who spotted the last one will be looking at a different report when it lands. Hand checking runs out of road quickly. Six measures put the checks on a page and give you a number to watch.

You have already written the first of them, Orphan Aircraft Rows, earlier in this chapter. Three more follow, then the two that roll all four into a card. Rename the tables and columns and they work anywhere. Put them on a hidden diagnostics page. The page the customer sees has no use for them.

DAXBlank Key Rows
Blank Station Keys =
CALCULATE (
    COUNTROWS ( FactWorkOrder ),
    ISBLANK ( FactWorkOrder[StationCode] )
)
CALCULATE
The predicate form, because the test touches one column and needs no row context. It is shorter and cheaper than a bare COUNTROWS wrapped in FILTER. CALCULATE is the only function that can evaluate an aggregate under an added filter.
Expansion
The predicate is expanded internally into a filter over ALL(FactWorkOrder[StationCode]), which means the measure ignores any station slicer on the page. That is what you want on a diagnostics page. Wrap the predicate in KEEPFILTERS if you want it to respect the slicer instead.
Limitation
ISBLANK is deliberately narrow and will not see an empty string, which is exactly what an Excel or CSV source often delivers. If your source can produce both, use the predicate FactWorkOrder[StationCode] = "", which is true for a blank and for an empty string, and accept that you have then lost the ability to tell the two apart in DAX. The clean separation only exists in Power Query, which is an argument for doing the test there.
Reading
1 on the raw Meridian work orders, WO-1005. 0 after the Power Query mapping, because the key is then the string UNASSIGNED and is no longer blank.
DAXDuplicate Key Count
Duplicate Aircraft Keys =
VAR KeyRows =
    ADDCOLUMNS (
        DISTINCT ( DimAircraft[ACREG] ),
        "@Rows", CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( DimAircraft ) )
    )
RETURN
    COUNTROWS ( FILTER ( KeyRows, [@Rows] > 1 ) )
ADDCOLUMNS
Over a key list rather than SUMMARIZE with an added column. SUMMARIZE applies a context transition to its extension columns in a way that is difficult to predict and that the published guidance advises against. ADDCOLUMNS with an explicit CALCULATE makes the transition visible in the code.
DISTINCT
DISTINCT and never VALUES, for the reason set out in the why box further down this section. Any key list inside a measure that hunts broken keys has to exclude the virtual blank member, or the measure starts reporting on the thing it was written to expose.
CALCULATE
Wrapping COUNTROWS is what performs the context transition. It turns the current key into a filter, so the count applies to that one key.
Alternative
COUNTROWS(DimAircraft) - DISTINCTCOUNT(DimAircraft[ACREG]) is faster and shorter, and it returns the number of excess rows where this measure returns the number of offending keys. On the un-deduplicated append that reads 4, and so does this measure, by coincidence. Do not rely on the coincidence.
DAXFact Rows Not Matching a Dimension
Parts Rows With No Header =
VAR PartKeys = DISTINCT ( FactParts[WorkOrderID] )
VAR HeaderKeys = DISTINCT ( FactWorkOrder[WorkOrderID] )
VAR Missing = EXCEPT ( PartKeys, HeaderKeys )
RETURN
    CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( FactParts ), Missing )
EXCEPT
Rather than FILTER with NOT CONTAINS. EXCEPT is a set operation the engine resolves in one pass and it preserves the data lineage of its first argument, which is what lets the result be used directly as a CALCULATE filter on line 6. The CONTAINS version performs a lookup per candidate key and has no lineage to reuse.
Line 6
Because Missing carries lineage on FactParts[WorkOrderID], passing it as a filter needs no TREATAS. Adding one here would be harmless and redundant.
Independence
This measure works without the relationship, so it still returns a number when the relationship was never created or is inactive, which is exactly when you most need one.
Reading
1 on Meridian, P-9007. Change COUNTROWS to SUMX(FactParts, FactParts[Qty] * FactParts[UnitCost]) and it reads 4,900, which is the money at stake.
Why this and not that

Use DISTINCT in that measure, even though DISTINCT and VALUES are usually interchangeable. VALUES returns the virtual blank row that the engine adds to the one side of an invalid relationship, and DISTINCT leaves it out. FactWorkOrder is the one side of the header to line relationships, so it already carries a blank member because of P-9007. Use VALUES for HeaderKeys and the blank row joins the set of valid headers, which is the defect quietly certifying itself as clean. When you are writing a measure whose whole job is to find broken keys, never use the function that hides them.

DAXModel Health
Model Health Issues =
COALESCE (
    [Orphan Aircraft Rows]
        + [Blank Station Keys]
        + [Duplicate Aircraft Keys]
        + [Parts Rows With No Header],
    0
)

Model Health =
VAR Issues = [Model Health Issues]
RETURN
    SWITCH (
        TRUE (),
        Issues = 0, "Keys clean",
        Issues <= 5, Issues & " rows to review",
        Issues & " rows failing key checks"
    )
COALESCE
All four inputs are a COUNTROWS over a table that is empty on a healthy model, and COUNTROWS over an empty table returns blank. Blank plus blank stays blank in DAX, so the sum on a fully clean model would return blank and the card would print nothing at all on the one day you wanted to see a zero. COALESCE wraps the whole sum once and turns that case into 0. Wrapping each of the four separately would say the same thing four times.
Two measures
A numeric one and a text one. The numeric measure is what conditional formatting rules, alerts and any future aggregation can consume. The text measure is only for a card a human reads. Ship the text version alone and you cannot set a data alert on model health, which is usually the thing you actually wanted.
SWITCH
With TRUE() as the expression, rather than nested IF. The semantics are identical, conditions are tested in order until one holds, but the branches sit at one indent level instead of three, and adding a fifth check later does not require rebalancing brackets.
Reading
On the raw Meridian sources: 1 plus 1 plus 0 plus 1 equals 3. After building DimAircraft from the union and mapping the blank station: 0 plus 0 plus 0 plus 1 equals 1, the WO-1009 orphan that Meridian keeps on purpose.
Orphan aircraft rows
1 raw, 0 fixed
Blank station keys
1 raw, 0 fixed
Duplicate dimension keys
0 fixed, 4 if the append is not deduplicated
Parts rows with no header
1, worth 4,900
Performance note

Three of these measures scan a fact table with no filter applied, and one of them does it inside a FILTER that cannot be pushed into the storage engine cleanly. On Meridian's 8 and 7 row facts that is free. On a 40 million row parts table it is a query that runs every time someone opens the page. Either move the checks into Power Query so they are evaluated once at refresh and loaded as a small results table, or keep them on a page that is hidden and only opened deliberately. A diagnostics page that slows down the report is a diagnostics page that gets deleted.

Where each defect should be fixed

You now have a fix for all nine defects. The remaining question is where each fix lives, and it matters more than it sounds. The trim on TECH-04 can go in three different places, and only one of them stops the same trailing space arriving again next month.

Three layers are available: the source system, Power Query, and the model. The general rule is that you fix as far upstream as you can influence, because a defect fixed in a report is a defect that will reappear. The trailing space on TECH-04 came from a timesheet screen with no input validation. Trim it in Power Query and it is gone from your model, and still there next month, and still there in the payroll extract that a different team is about to build.

Two rows in that table are worth arguing about. Case normalisation belongs in Power Query and nowhere else, because the model treats case-different values as equal and therefore cannot report the discrepancy back to you. And the orphan header key belongs in the source alone, because there is no correct value for you to invent.

The bottom row breaks the rule this section opens with. You cannot push an unmapped crew code further upstream, because upstream of CrewCodeMap there is only you. Where the rule still bites is the input to your table. Ask the resource scheduler to tell you when a crew code is opened, and the defect moves one layer up to somebody who sees it before you do.

Interview-level insight

Hiding a data quality problem and resolving one look identical in the report and are opposites in the business. Mapping WO-1009's parts to an unknown work order makes the station matrix add to 28,245 and the page stops looking broken. The 4,900 of hydraulics that left the store against a work order nobody can find is still unaccounted for, and now it has a label that implies somebody accounted for it. Resolution means the defect count falls because the underlying record was corrected or written off. Concealment means the defect count falls because you changed where the number is displayed. The test is simple: after your change, can anyone still find out how many rows failed, and who owns them? If the answer is no, you removed the evidence and left the model exactly where it was.

What to remember
  • An unmatched key on the many side adds one virtual blank row to the one side and every unmatched fact row is attributed to it. Grand totals stay correct, breakdowns do not, and there is only ever one blank row no matter how many distinct keys are broken.
  • Two visuals disagreeing by a fixed amount, 23,345 against 28,245, is the standard signature of an orphan key. The visual that groups by an attribute behind the broken join loses those rows. The visual with no such grouping keeps them.
  • A blank member is indistinguishable from a join failure, so map genuine absences to an explicit member such as UNASSIGNED and recover real identifiers such as N338WA into the dimension with unknown attributes.
  • Keys that look identical are tested with Text.Length or LEN, never by eye. 7 and 8 on the same displayed TECH-04 is the whole diagnosis, and 4.5 hours is the whole cost.
  • Power Query is case sensitive, the model's relationships are not. Do not rely on either. Normalise case explicitly on both sides of every key.
  • Duplicate keys on the one side stop the relationship being created and fail the refresh once it exists. Find them with Group By and a count over 1, and normalise case before you group.
  • A foreign key can be valid in every system that holds it and still fail, when the table it has to join to is one you maintain by hand. CRW-XX9 is a real crew code that CrewCodeMap is missing, and the 10.0 hours behind it sit in an unnamed service line with no error raised anywhere. Nothing upstream will fix it, so write the unmapped check yourself and label the gap Unmapped so it shows on the page.
  • Fix as far upstream as your influence reaches. A defect fixed in a report reappears at the next refresh, in the next report, and in the next team's extract.
